This ground breaking article first published in 1955 reviews the advancements in psychotherapy research over sixty years, focusing on the development of sound recording and content analysis techniques. The article discusses how these innovations have addressed key obstacles in the field, such as the transient nature of psychotherapy sessions and the subjective interpretation of data. It also reviews a variety of methodological studies, content-analysis techniques, and theoretical frameworks, including those influenced by psychoanalysis and behavior theory. Key findings suggest that content analysis has significantly contributed to understanding client-therapist interactions, particularly in non-directive therapy settings. The article emphasizes the need for continued integration of psychological theory with empirical research to refine therapeutic practices.

A fascinating read for anybody interested in the history and practice of psychotherapy.

It is only sixty years since Freud developed psychoanalysis (Jones, 1953) and thereby originated dynamic psychotherapy. Although psychoanalysts and other investigators have learned a great deal about psychotherapy in these sixty years, studies of psychotherapy have suffered from three hindrances:

  1. The basic data of psychotherapy were transient. Furthermore, they were accessible only to the therapist; others had to take his word for what happened in the interviews. The consequences of this lack of adequate recording are eloquently set forth by Kubie (1947).

  2. Conclusions stemming from investigations were matters of impression and opinion, because there was no technique for studying the verbal materials objectively.

  3. The data could not be fitted into a suitable theoretical framework because the psychologies of the day (for example, Wundt's and Brentano's) had little relevance to the phenomena observed. To appreciate this, one might imagine oneself in Freud's place in 1895 and ask, "How could I explain the things I've observed?"

Recent methodological and theoretical developments seem to justify the hope that these three obstacles to scientific research on psychotherapy can be overcome. The new methods are sound recording and content analysis; the new theoretical developments are the recent attempts to develop a general science of behavior. Sound recording of interviews has made a common set of data available to scientists, a set that can be preserved and be studied as many times as necessary (Brody, Newman, & Redlich, 1951; Covner, 1942, 1944; Gill, Newman, & Redlich, 1954; Porter, 1943a, 1943b; Rogers, 1942a, 1942b; Robinson, 1950; Zinn, Lasswell, 1938, 1949). Pioneers in the recording of psychotherapy interviews include Zinn, Lasswell (1938, 1949), Rogers (1942a, 1942b), Robinson (1950), Covner (1942, 1944), and Porter (1943a, 1943b). All of the studies reviewed in this article, with one exception, derived their data from sound recordings.

Content analysis is a method for studying the content of communication in an objective, systematic, and quantitative way (Berelson, 1952). "Content" is what is said. Lasswell (1938, 1949) pioneered the application of content analysis to social science problems. In the past decade, content analysis has been widely used for studying therapeutic interviews. All the studies reviewed in this paper are content-analysis studies.

General theories of behavior, which have been developed in recent years, provide possible frameworks for explaining the data of psychotherapy and for guiding investigators toward studies that will advance our understanding of human behavior as well as give answers to specific questions (Hilgard, 1952). The fusion of psychological theory with psychotherapeutic research is exemplified in the work of Bollard and Miller (1950). Although the nondirective group at first had no comprehensive theoretical platform, in recent years these investigators have shown a growing tendency to state explicit theories and to design their investigations to test hypotheses derived from the theories (Rogers, 1951; Rogers & Dymond, 1954). And, of course, it cannot be forgotten that the psychoanalysts, building on their clinical experience, constructed a comprehensive psychological theory. Despite its vagueness and lack of rigorous formulation, it has served as a guide for a number of investigators.

In this paper we attempt to review the considerable body of literature on content analysis of recorded interviews.

A Survey of the Studies

The content-analysis studies of psychotherapy fall into three general classes: (a) Methodological studies, in which the aim was principally to develop measures, (b) Descriptive studies of cases, and (c) Theoretically guided studies of therapy (i.e., studies of cause-and-effect relationships). Although it is admitted that the third kind of study, if well done, contributes the most to our understanding, studies of the first type are necessary to develop methods and studies of the second type may provide hunches that can be rigorously tested later by a theoretically oriented study.

Methodological Studies

In these studies, the emphasis is on development of new measures rather than on testing of hypotheses. Nevertheless, the inventor of a measure always has some theoretical presuppositions; and these are what influence him to measure one kind of thing rather than another. Porter (1943a, 1943b), for instance, developed a classification of therapist responses. His classification emphasizes the degree to which the therapist takes responsibility for the interview, which Porter (1950) considers an important variable in therapy. Among the nondirective therapists, Royer (1943), Snyder (1945), Curran (1945), Raimy (1948), Hogan (1952), Haigh (1949), Stock (1949), Hoffman (1949), Kahn (1953), and others have developed measures of various kinds. We shall choose several measures for special comment, because they illustrate the problems involved in such classifications.

Snyder's System

Snyder's system (1945) has been especially influential. Seeman (1949), Aronson (1953), Rakusin (1953), Tucker (1953), Gillespie (1953), and Blau (1953) have made use of it in their investigations. In Snyder's system, the therapist's responses are classified according to a modification of Porter's categories. The categories designate the technique which the therapist uses: restating content, clarifying feeling, interpreting, structuring, leading, suggesting, questioning, persuading, accepting, reassuring, approving, disapproving. The client's responses are classified under these headings: problems, simple responses (questions, answers, acceptance, disagreement), insight, planning. Notice that Snyder, like Porter, considers the degree of responsibility assumed by the therapist to be an important variable in the therapist's behavior. On the side of the client, Snyder's classification implies that it is important to notice whether the client is discussing problems or showing understanding. Presumably, in successful cases, problems dominate at the beginning and insight and plans dominate at the end.

Curran's Two Systems

Curran (1945) has reported a detailed analysis of a single case. His study is of special interest for two reasons. First, he measured "insight" by noting instances of the client's connecting two different problems. Also of interest is Curran's classification of the problems discussed by the client. The problems discussed by the client included: hostility, dependency, insecurity, unhappiness, conflict, discouragement, withdrawal, daydreaming, feelings of inferiority, sex, sin, younger brother, school work, and war. The special interest of this classification lies in the fact that while most researchers have only noted whether the client talked about a problem or about something else, Curran described what the problems were that the client talked about.

Haigh's Measure of Defensiveness

Haigh (1949) developed a measure of defensiveness, building on previous work by Hogan (1952). Haigh defined defensive behavior as behavior that shifts attention away from an inconsistency that, if perceived, would threaten the client. It may be noted, by the way, that in order to judge a remark as defensive according to Haigh's system, one must make the judgment that two of the statements of the client are incongruent or that the client "ought" to be exploring some aspect of his emotional life and is avoiding it (Haigh, 1949, p. 182).

Interaction Process Analysis

The theory behind this system, which was developed by Bales (1950), is that problem solving involves a series of steps: getting information, making decisions, carrying out actions. At each stage of problem solving, the participants interact with each other. One participant, for instance, asks for information; another gives information; then a third member of the group may offer an opinion of the correctness of this information; the individual who gave the information may then proceed to defend his views with some heat. As applied to therapy, the Bales system is used to score both the remarks of the therapist and those of the client.

Discomfort-Relief Quotient

The Discomfort-Relief Quotient, or D.R.Q., was suggested by behavior theory. Behavior theory states that responses are incited by drives and reinforced by drive reduction. The learning of new habits is, according to this theory, accompanied by drive reduction. The new learning that occurs in successful therapy ought, therefore, to be accompanied by a reduction in drive. Guided by these theoretical notions, Dollard and Mowrer (1947) attempted to measure the amount of drive borne by the client. They did this by classifying each word as a drive, reward, or neutral word according to whether the word seemed to represent discomfort (suffering, tension, pain, unhappiness) or relief (comfort, satisfaction, enjoyment). The D.R.Q. is obtained by dividing the number of discomfort words by the total number of discomfort and relief words. The authors demonstrated very satisfactory reliability of D.R.Q. scores. Dollard and Mowrer, employing other units besides words, found that sentences and "thought units" could also be reliably scored.

Dollard and Mowrer intended the D.R.Q. as a measure of the tension experienced by the client. We know, however, that the client's verbal responses do not always accurately label his drives (Dollard & Miller, 1950). It is possible that: (a) A verbal response may indicate a drive when the drive is not present, as when a client makes an insincere complaint in order to enlist the sympathy of the therapist, (b) A drive may be present with no verbal response describing it, as when a client denies having sexual wishes while his other behavior convinces us that he does have them, (c) A verbal response may describe a conflict different from the one the patient is actually experiencing, as when talk about vocational problems replaces talk about an unconscious homosexual conflict. It is also possible that the importance of a drive is not accurately reflected by the frequency of occurrence of sentences about it. It is the task of empirical research to discover whether clients accurately label their drives or whether these possible distorting factors interfere with accurate labeling to such a degree that verbal responses cannot be used as indices of drive.

In a study of interviews from six recorded psychotherapy cases, Mowrer et al. (1953) found a moderately high relationship between the D.R.Q. scores for interviews and measures of palmar sweating made after the same interviews. For five of these cases, the correlation between D.R.Q. and palmar sweating was .57; in the sixth case, for which the correlation was computed separately, the correlation was only .30. To the extent that palmar sweating is itself an adequate measure of "tension," this finding somewhat strengthens the case for considering the D.R.Q. a measure of tension. Meadow et al. (1952), studying psychotic patients, found no significant relationship between the D.R.Q. score in a special interview and a psychiatrist's rating of tension made on the basis of other interviews with the patient. In interpreting this result, one should note, first, that the rating of tension was not made in the same situation in which the D.R.Q. score was obtained; second, that the result of a study of psychotic patients, who as a group are likely to make verbal responses without showing "appropriate" emotional reactions, may not predict very well what will be found when normal and neurotic subjects are studied; and, finally, that the validity of the psychiatrist's ratings is not known.

The problem of how the verbal behavior of the client, which is taken note of in content analysis, is related to his nonverbal behavior, is a difficulty not only for the D.R.Q. but also for every other system of content analysis.

Positive-Negative-Ambivalent Quotient

A measure independently developed by Raimy (1948) has a strong family resemblance to the D.R.Q. Raimy's PNAvQ is a quotient, as the D.R.Q. is; and, like the D.R.Q., it is an indication of positive and negative emotional reactions of the client. It differs from the D.R.Q. in focusing attention on the statements of the client about himself (whereas the D.R.Q. includes all statements of the client, whether they are self-referential or not). In some cases, the two measures would be expected to give similar results, which is what Kauffman and Raimy (1949) found to be true in a study of 17 counseling interviews. These authors also found the PNAvQ to be somewhat lower, on the average, than the D.R.Q.

Horwitz (1951), however, found only a low correlation (r = .38) between the D.R.Q. and PNAvQ in 36 initial casework interviews. It is possible that the low correlation should be attributed to the unreliability of scoring; but this seems unlikely, since investigators using these measures in other studies have found them to be reliable. It is more likely that the correlation is low because the D.R.Q. includes all statements of the client, whether about himself or about others, while the PNAvQ includes only statements referring to the client. If the client talks a great deal about other persons, these statements will affect the D.R.Q. but not the PNAvQ — with a resulting drop in agreement between the two measures.

Doubting that a measure of the "self-concept" would be much related to the D.R.Q. if the "self-concept" were narrowly defined, Horwitz also investigated the correlation between the D.R.Q. and a "self-approval quotient." The self-approval quotient is the same as the PNAvQ except that statements which indicate that the client is happy, glad, or improving, or fearful, anxious, or unsuccessful are not included. (Such statements are included in the PNAvQ.) The only statements included in the self-approval quotient are those in which the client evaluates himself. The D.R.Q. was not significantly correlated with the self-approval quotient (r = .14).

A System in Terms of Motivation and Conflict

Murray (1954) devised a system for studying motivation and conflict in psychotherapy, influenced by psychoanalysis and learning theory. Murray wished to designate underlying motivations of the client but also wanted the system to be objective, with explicit rules for inferring the underlying motives. To maintain objectivity, Murray chose categories that required relatively little inference by the scorer and reflected mostly manifest content. The category system designates statements expressing a need, statements expressing anxiety about a need, and statements expressing hostility due to frustration of a need. The drives included are: sex, affection, dependence, independence, and an "unspecified" drive. The system also requires the scorer to designate the person who is the object of the need (e.g., the person who is loved or hated).

Murray (1954) also devised categories for describing the therapist's behavior, building on the theoretical work of Dollard and Miller (1950). Therapist remarks were classified according to activity-passivity and their function as rewards, punishments, labels, discriminations, generalizations, instructions concerning free association, directions, or probes.

The reliability of scoring these categories was studied by Murray (1954) and found to be fairly high. However, reliability decreased when more simultaneous discriminations were required from the scorer, a finding that Kaplan and Goldsen (1949) also observed.

Lasswell's General Purpose System

Lasswell (1938) suggested a scheme for classifying interview materials under general headings so that the system can be applied to diverse interviews. The classification tells who makes the statement (talker-listener-another), whom the statement refers to (talker-listener-another), and what attitude is expressed (favorable-unfavorable). To our knowledge, this scheme has not been extensively applied to psychotherapy cases, except in a recent study by Rosenman (1953).

Other Measures

Other proposed measures include Collier's (1953) scale for the degree of "uncovering" used by the therapist, Miller's (1949) measure of "acceptance," Raskin's (1952) "locus-of-evaluation" rating, Elton's (1950) responsibility rating, Carnes and Robinson's (1948) talk ratio, and White's (1947) frustration-satisfaction ratio, a measure similar to the D.R.Q.

Descriptive Studies

Movement from Problems to Insight

Snyder (1945, 1947) described how the content of the client's speech changes during the course of nondirective therapy. According to Snyder, clients begin by stating their problems, then progress to discussing these problems with increased insight, and finally move to formulating plans for new responses. Studying four "successful" cases and one "unsuccessful" case, Snyder (1947) found that the successful cases demonstrated a clear trend from problems to plans, while the unsuccessful case did not. Seeman (1949) repeated Snyder's study using a larger sample of cases and confirmed his results. Similarly, Blau (1953) and Snyder (1945) both found that clients in successful cases talked less about problems and more about insights and plans at the end of treatment than at the beginning. In addition, clients expressed more positive feelings and fewer negative feelings as therapy progressed. However, such changes were not observed in unsuccessful cases. While these studies specify some ways in which client speech behavior changes during therapy, they do not independently confirm whether the cases judged to be successful were indeed successful, since the judgments of success were likely influenced by these same speech changes.

Attitudes Toward Self

Raimy (1948) studied the types of attitudes toward the self expressed by clients at different stages of therapy. He found that in cases judged to be successful, clients' positive statements about themselves increased while negative statements decreased as therapy progressed. Seeman (1949) examined all client attitudes, including those about others, and found that positive expressions increased while negative expressions decreased throughout therapy. Interestingly, Seeman found no significant correlation between this change and the counselor’s rating of case success when considering all statements of attitude. However, when he focused only on statements expressed in the present tense, there was a significant correlation (r = .66) between attitude change and the counselor's rating of success. Bugental (1952) also used Raimy's scales and made some modifications in his studies.

Studies Using the D.R.Q.

Another approach to studying the course of psychotherapy involves noting changes in the degree of tension expressed by the client’s sentences, as measured by the Discomfort-Relief Quotient (D.R.Q.), described earlier. Plunt (1947, 1948, 1949) found little agreement between changes in D.R.Q. from the beginning to the end of casework and the degree of "movement" or progress in the case, as judged by experienced caseworkers. Even when judgments of experienced caseworkers were quantified and made highly reliable using a "movement" scale, there was little agreement between movement and the drop in D.R.Q. Assum and Levy (1948) studied one case in which they found a drop in D.R.Q. accompanied by success. Rogers (1951) studied three cases—one judged as very successful, one as moderately successful, and one as unsuccessful. The successful cases showed a drop in D.R.Q., while the unsuccessful one did not. Cofer and Chance (1950) computed the D.R.Q. for five published cases (four nondirective and one psychoanalytic), and all showed a drop in D.R.Q. as judged by the therapists. Mowrer (1953) also reported one of his own successful cases that demonstrated a drop in D.R.Q.

Although some investigators have found a drop in D.R.Q. associated with success (based on the analysis of verbatim transcripts of 10 cases), others (who analyzed caseworkers' notes of 38 cases) found no relationship between D.R.Q. decrease and success. The studies reporting positive results did not report the reliability of the judgments of success, but reliability was established in the 38 cases where no relationship was found. Murray, Auld, and White (1954) reported a "partly successful" case that showed no drop in D.R.Q., leading to a discussion about why the D.R.Q. did not change.

A critical issue in these studies is the lack of an adequate measure of "success." Until such a measure is devised, it is difficult to assess whether changes in the D.R.Q. correlate with therapy success.

Relations of Problems to Each Other

Curran (1945) analyzed 20 recorded interviews from a single case and described how the client’s perception of relationships between their problems changed over time. Initially, the client discussed a large number of problems separately. By the end of therapy, the client discussed fewer problems and began combining problems that had previously been separate. Curran contributed to methodology by introducing the idea of measuring "insight" through the client's discovery of connections between previously unconnected problems.

Differences Between Different Therapies

Porter (1943a, 1943b) studied differences between "directive" and "nondirective" counselors. He found that directive counselors talked more than nondirective counselors and assumed more responsibility for guiding the interview by asking questions, explaining, probing, and offering advice. Gump (1944) compared the responses of therapists in one psychoanalytic case and five nondirective cases, noting differences in their techniques, though no generalizations about analytic or nondirective therapy can be drawn from only six cases.

Strupp (1955) compared the verbal responses of Rogerian and non-Rogerian therapists by asking them how they would respond to various client statements. Using Bales' system for classification, Strupp found differences between the groups, but also noted substantial variation within each group. Strupp (1954) further categorized non-Rogerian therapists based on whether they had received personal analysis. Those who had been analyzed were more likely to disagree with or show antagonism toward clients' statements, and less likely to show passive acceptance, compared to those who had not been analyzed.

Group Psychotherapy

Coffey, Freedman, Leary, and Ossorio (1950) described the content of group psychotherapy interviews with college students. The authors identified the core conflict in these groups as a combination of high intellectual ideals and emotional inhibition, resulting in passivity, guilt, and emotional stagnation. Group discussions focused on sexuality (27% of total time), vocational problems (14%), attitudes toward psychotherapy (12%), self-perception (11%), and other topics. Roberts and Strodtbeck (1953) applied the Bales system to group therapy sessions with paranoid schizophrenic and depressive patients, finding differences in negative responses and leader-focused interaction. Depressed patients responded less frequently than schizophrenic patients, as expected.

Gorlow, Hoch, and Telschow (1952) studied nondirective group therapy sessions using categories similar to Snyder’s, while Perry and Estes (1953) applied Bales' Interaction Process Analysis system to a student counseling case, documenting shifts in counselor techniques and client responses over four sessions. Perry and Estes interpreted this shift as the counselor moving from nondirective techniques to a more collaborative approach, and the student relinquishing expectations of authoritarian guidance from the counselor.

Studies of Linguistic Characteristics

Although not covered extensively in this paper, several studies have examined the grammatical structure of clients’ speech during therapy. Studies by Roshal (1953), Zimmerman and Langdon (1953), and Grummon (1950) are notable examples. Mowrer (1953) provides a comprehensive review of this research.

Theoretically Guided Studies

Lasswell's Pioneering Work

Lasswell (1935) was a pioneer not only in making sound recordings of analytic interviews but also in applying content analysis methods to study them. In his earliest report on the analysis of transcribed interviews, Lasswell (1935) classified the client's utterances based on whether they referred to the interviewer or not. He proposed the hypothesis that conscious affect is indicated by references to the interviewer, whereas unconscious tension is indicated by signs such as slow speech and pauses. If this hypothesis is correct, tension—evidenced by slow speech—should correlate with other measures, such as physiological indicators of tension. Lasswell found a correlation between verbal indices of tension (e.g., slow speech) and physiological indices (e.g., high skin conductivity), while references to the interviewer correlated with increased heart rate. Lasswell interpreted these results as supporting his hypothesis regarding the relationship between conscious affect and references to the therapist. Furthermore, measures of unconscious tension (e.g., slow speech, high skin conductivity) were negatively correlated with conscious affect (e.g., references to the therapist, increased heart rate).

In a later study, Lasswell (1937) found similar results and included additional measures such as blood pressure before and after each session and the amount of bodily movement during the interview. His findings showed that unconscious tension decreased over time, while conscious affect increased, reflecting successful psychoanalytic treatment.

Is Clarification of Feeling the Best Technique?

Rogers (1944) believed that progress in therapy follows "recognition of feeling" by the therapist. In contrast, psychoanalysts argue that techniques such as interpreting resistance and labeling previously unrecognized emotions are essential (Freud, 1924; Dollard & Miller, 1950; Colby, 1951). Studies using content analysis have investigated the effectiveness of various therapist techniques. For example, Snyder (1945) found that "insight" and "discussion of plans" by the client were more likely to follow nondirective techniques by the therapist.

Sherman (1945) analyzed student counseling sessions and found that "tentative analysis" by the therapist was most likely to be followed by client responses rated high on her "working relationship" scale. "Interpretation" and "clarification" were moderately likely to be followed by good "working relationships," while "urging" was least likely. Sherman also examined the relationship between therapist techniques and client insight, finding that "clarification" was most likely to be followed by client insight, followed by "interpretation" and "tentative analysis." "Urging" was the least effective in eliciting insight. However, Sherman's definition of "insight" may differ from that of other investigators, particularly psychoanalysts, who often require the patient to uncover unconscious motivations to be credited with insight.

Bergman (1951) reported that "reflection of feeling" was the only technique that consistently led to insight or continued self-exploration. Interpretation, on the other hand, tended to halt exploration. Gillespie (1953), however, found that client resistance, including hostility toward the therapist and therapy, was more likely to occur following therapist interpretation or inaccurate clarification of feeling, though not after accurate clarification or restatement of content. Other signs of resistance (e.g., long pauses, changing the subject, emotional blocking) were not significantly related to interpretative activity. Psychoanalysts view such signs of resistance as important indicators of unresolved conflict (Colby, 1951), although nondirective therapists may place less emphasis on these overt expressions of resistance.

Dittmann (1952) studied a single case and found that client responses indicating progress were most likely to follow therapist responses that were slightly more interpretive than pure "reflection."

What Can We Learn from These Studies?

The research, particularly that of Robinson and his students, shows that no single therapist response type is universally effective. The effectiveness of a therapist's response likely depends on various factors, including the client's expectations (e.g., whether the client expects advice), the specific circumstances, and the client's readiness for increased self-awareness at a given moment. Additionally, these studies reveal the importance of defining what constitutes "effectiveness." Different measures of client insight and progress yield different conclusions about what makes therapy effective. Measures favored by nondirective therapists may not be considered appropriate by psychoanalysts, and vice versa.

Differences in Therapist Responses

Reid and Snyder (1947) studied differences between counselors by replaying phonographic recordings of client statements to a group of 15 clinical psychologists. After hearing each client statement, the psychologists were asked to write down their interpretation of the client’s feelings. Reid and Snyder found a wide range of responses to the same client statements. More experienced counselors, who were judged by their instructors as more skillful, were more likely to interpret client feelings in line with the majority. Additionally, some counselors had consistent tendencies to interpret clients' feelings in a preferred way. For instance, some consistently perceived clients as insecure, while others labeled the same statements as hopeful or ambitious. This study demonstrates an experimental method for studying therapist behavior.

Haigh's Study of Defensiveness

Haigh (1949) hypothesized that "defensiveness" (incorrect perception of one’s behavior) decreases during successful psychotherapy. In his study of 10 client-centered therapy cases, he counted defensive responses in each session and found fewer defensive responses in the second half of treatment compared to the first half in seven cases. In two cases, defensive responses increased in the second half, and in one case, there were no identified defensive responses. While this small sample does not allow for definitive conclusions, the trend suggests that defensiveness may decrease as therapy progresses.

A further goal of Haigh's study was to show that increased awareness of defensiveness leads to its reduction. Haigh suggested that as clients became aware of inconsistencies in their behavior, they were able to abandon defensive opinions. He reported that in the cases where defensive behavior decreased, clients became increasingly aware of their defensiveness, while in cases where defensive behavior increased, clients remained unaware of their defensive behavior. While these findings are tentative, they suggest that awareness of defensiveness may play a role in its reduction.

A Study of Hostility and Defenses

Murray (1954) conducted an exploratory study using his content-analysis system to test hypotheses drawn from psychoanalytic theory and learning theory. In this study, he found that defensive statements decreased while hostile statements increased as therapy progressed. Furthermore, interviews with a large number of defensive statements tended to have fewer hostile statements (r = -.73). The defensive statements included intellectualization and physical complaints, while hostile statements involved expressions of frustration and resentment.

Murray interpreted the findings through learning theory, suggesting that defenses were motivated by anxiety. When a client used a defensive statement instead of a hostile one, they escaped the anxiety that would accompany the expression of hostility. Over time, as the therapist interpreted certain defenses, such as physical complaints, and maintained a permissive attitude toward hostility, the defenses weakened, and hostility was more freely expressed.

Murray also tested the displacement theory, which predicts that hostility will first be expressed toward less significant people and then toward more important individuals (e.g., a parent). However, his data showed the reverse: hostility was first directed at more significant people and later toward less significant individuals. This led Murray and Berkun (1955) to revise displacement theory, which they further tested in an animal experiment.

Murray also observed that the two defenses, intellectual discussion and physical complaints, functioned as members of a habit-family hierarchy. When one defense declined, the other increased, providing additional evidence that defenses operate within a habit-family structure.

Attitudes Toward Self and Others

Nondirective researchers have investigated changes in clients’ attitudes toward themselves and others during therapy. One hypothesis is that the therapist’s warm, accepting attitude helps clients become more aware of their own wishes and behaviors, leading to greater self-acceptance (Rogers, 1951). A second hypothesis posits that as clients feel better about themselves, they can develop more positive feelings toward others. Finally, the third hypothesis suggests that individuals who feel positively toward others will get along with them better (Rogers, 1951).

The second hypothesis was tested by Sheerer (1949) and Stock (1949), both of whom found a correlation between positive attitudes toward oneself and positive attitudes toward others. Mclntyre (1952) investigated the third hypothesis but found no significant evidence that people who are accepting of others are also accepted by others, based on sociometric ratings.

Prognostic Studies

Lasswell (1936) explored whether certain speech and physiological measures in early interviews could predict the success of therapy. He reported that a combination of these early measures allowed accurate predictions of client progress. However, Lasswell's sample was small, and this promising work requires further replication with larger samples.

Page (1953) examined the variability of clients’ speech in their first interviews to predict therapy outcomes but found no significant correlation with success. However, the amount of client talk in the first session had a small but significant correlation (r = .30) with success.

Choice of a Content-Analysis System

Choosing a content-analysis system involves selecting a relevant topic and appropriate methods. Validity is a key concern when deciding which system to use. For instance, if the researcher is interested in emotions, they will want to ensure that a system like the D.R.Q. accurately measures emotion.

Janis (1949) proposed that the validity of a content-analysis system should be judged by the number of relationships it can uncover. A valid system should show significant correlations with other valid measures when theoretically appropriate. However, it is not necessary for a measure to correlate with every valid measure, as some variables may not be directly related to the outcomes of interest.
