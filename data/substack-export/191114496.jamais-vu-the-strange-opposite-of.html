Most people have heard of déjà vu.

It’s that strange moment when a situation feels uncannily familiar, as if you’ve somehow experienced it before. Even though your logical mind tells you it can’t be true, the feeling can be powerful enough to make you pause and wonder what just happened.

Psychologists have been intrigued by déjà vu for years because it offers a glimpse into how the brain handles memory and recognition.

But did you know that déjà vu has an opposite?

It’s called jamais vu, a French phrase that translates as “never seen.” And instead of making the new feel familiar, it does the reverse. Something you know perfectly well suddenly feels unfamiliar, strange, or somehow wrong.

An Early Observation From Margaret Floy Washburn

Although the term jamais vu may sound unfamiliar, the experience it describes has appeared in the psychological literature for more than a century.

In 1907, the psychologist Margaret Floy Washburn, working with one of her students, Elizabeth Severance, reported an unusual effect that could occur when people stared at a word for several minutes. Over time, the word began to look strange and fragmented, as though it had somehow lost its meaning.

Washburn and Severance described this effect as a “loss of associative power.”

In other words, the mental connections that normally give a word its sense of meaning seemed to weaken with repetition or prolonged attention.

Experiences like this hint at something interesting about familiarity. The sense that a word, object, or place is known to us is not simply stored in memory. It depends on mental processes that normally operate smoothly in the background but can occasionally behave in unexpected ways.

Inducing Jamais Vu In The Laboratory

More than a century after Washburn’s observation, psychologists began investigating the phenomenon more systematically.

In a series of experiments, researchers Akira O’Connor and Christopher Moulin explored whether the experience of jamais vu could be deliberately triggered under controlled conditions.

Their approach was surprisingly simple.

Participants were asked to repeatedly write the same word again and again. Some copied common words such as door, while others wrote the word the, one of the most familiar words in the English language.

At first the task felt straightforward. But after enough repetitions, participants began to stop the task.

Many reported stopping because something about the word no longer seemed right to them.

In the first experiment, around 70 percent of participants reported experiencing this strange sensation at least once. It typically occurred after about one minute of repetition, or roughly thirty repetitions of the same word.

What Jamais Vu Actually Feels Like

In describing their experience, many participants reported that the word they were writing suddenly seemed unfamiliar, even though they knew it should not.

One participant wrote that the words “lose their meaning the more you look at them.”

Others described something closer to visual strangeness. A familiar word could begin to look wrong or unfamiliar despite being spelled correctly. As one participant put it, “It doesn’t seem right, almost looks like it’s not really a word but someone’s tricked me into thinking it is.”

Another participant reported that the normally automatic act of writing began to feel unusual, saying they “seemed to lose control of hand.”

Descriptions like these capture the peculiar quality of jamais vu. Participants still recognized the word and understood its meaning, yet the sense of familiarity that normally accompanies that recognition briefly disappeared.

For a moment, something completely familiar felt strangely unfamiliar.

Why not have a go yourself? Take a common word such as door or the and write it repeatedly on a piece of paper for a minute or so, just as the participants in the experiment did. I’d love to know if you experienced jamais vu.

When The Mind Signals Something Has Become Too Automatic

One of the most interesting ideas emerging from this research is that jamais vu may serve a useful psychological purpose.

According to O’Connor and Moulin, the strange feeling that something familiar has suddenly become unfamiliar may act as a kind of mental warning signal.

When we repeat the same action over and over again, our cognitive processes can become extremely fluent and automatic. Normally this efficiency is helpful. It allows us to perform everyday tasks without having to think about every step.

But excessive repetition can also create problems.

If a task becomes too automatic, the mind risks drifting into a kind of cognitive autopilot. In those moments, attention may weaken and errors become more likely.

Jamais vu may function as a way of interrupting that process.

The sudden sense that something is wrong or unfamiliar forces us to pause and re-examine what we are doing. In that sense, the eerie feeling of unreality may actually act as a kind of reality check for the brain.

Rather than being a malfunction, the experience may be part of the mind’s way of maintaining flexibility and preventing us from becoming trapped in overly repetitive patterns of thought or action.

Why Repetition Can Make Words Lose Their Meaning

One explanation is a cognitive process known as satiation. When a word or idea is repeated over and over again, the mental representation associated with it can become temporarily overloaded. Instead of reinforcing meaning, excessive repetition can cause that representation to weaken, making the word feel strange or unfamiliar.

This idea is closely related to semantic satiation, a phenomenon in which repeated exposure to a word causes it to briefly lose its sense of meaning.

Another related effect is known as the verbal transformation effect. When a word is repeated continuously, listeners sometimes begin to hear entirely different words emerging from the repetition. For example, hearing the word “tress” repeated over and over can lead people to report hearing “dress,” “stress,” or other similar sounding words.

These effects suggest that repetition does not simply reinforce familiarity. Under certain conditions, it can disrupt the mental processes that normally give words their meaning.

It may be the case, therefore, that the strange feeling associated with jamais vu reflects a temporary breakdown in the systems that normally allow us to recognize familiar things effortlessly.

Jamais Vu Is Not Just About Words

The word-repetition experiment described above provided researchers with a simple way to trigger jamais vu under controlled laboratory conditions. But the phenomenon itself is not limited to language.

In discussing the experience more broadly, Akira O’Connor and Christopher Moulin note that jamais vu can arise in several familiar situations.

Jamais vu may involve looking at a familiar face and finding it suddenly unusual or unknown. Musicians have it momentarily – losing their way in a very familiar passage of music. You may have had it going to a familiar place and becoming disorientated or seeing it with “new eyes”.

These examples suggest that jamais vu is not simply about words losing their meaning. Instead, it’s about a temporary disruption in the processes that normally generate our sense of familiarity.

The unsettling sense that something is suddenly “not quite right” may therefore act as a kind of internal warning signal, that encourages us to re-examine what we are doing.

If this interpretation is found to be correct, jamais vu may simply be the mind’s way of maintaining cognitive flexibility, in order to prevent us from becoming trapped in overly repetitive patterns of thought or behavior.

Neurological Context

Although jamais vu is typically discussed as a curious quirk of everyday experience, similar sensations have also been reported in certain neurological contexts.

Unusual disturbances of familiarity are sometimes described in connection with temporal lobe epilepsy, a condition in which seizures affect brain regions involved in memory and recognition. Patients with temporal lobe epilepsy may report altered feelings of familiarity before or during a seizure, documenting experiences and sensations resembling jamais vu.

Episodes of unfamiliarity have also been noted in dissociative states, where a person may feel temporarily detached from their surroundings or from their own sense of self. In such cases, familiar people or places can briefly feel strangely unreal.

Extreme fatigue, prolonged concentration, or repetitive mental activity can also occasionally lead to moments in which something normally familiar suddenly feels oddly unfamiliar.

It’s important to note that in the vast majority of cases, experiences of jamais vu are brief, harmless, and nothing to worry about.

Other “Vu” Experiences

Jamais vu belongs to a small group of unusual experiences sometimes described as recognition anomalies. In each case, there is a mismatch between what we know and what we feel to be familiar.

These various experiences reveal that our sense of familiarity is not simply a record of the past stored in memory. It is a psychological signal generated by the brain, and occasionally that signal can behave in unexpected ways.

Final Thoughts

Most of the time we move through the world with complete confidence in our sense of familiarity. Words appear exactly as they should. Familiar places feel familiar. Faces are instantly recognizable. The mind rarely stops to question these everyday signals.

But moments like jamais vu reveal that this sense of familiarity is not as solid as it seems.

A word you have written hundreds of times can suddenly appear strange. A place you know well can briefly feel unfamiliar. For a moment, the ordinary world seems slightly out of alignment.

Our cognitive systems for recognizing what is familiar usually work flawlessly.

But every so often, a small glitch in that process reminds us that even the most ordinary sense of “knowing” depends on delicate mental mechanisms quietly operating beneath the surface of our awareness.

And occasionally, we get a fleeting glimpse of just how strange the familiar can become.
