I read a really interesting article this week. A team of researchers tracked over a hundred people’s daily activities and found that roughly two thirds of everything they did was triggered by habit rather than conscious choice.

Not just the obvious things, like brushing your teeth or putting on your seatbelt. The study captured people in real time throughout their day and found that most of their actions, from routine work tasks to what they ate and drank, were started automatically, without any deliberate decision.

I thought it would be interesting to explore how much of our day we’re actually present for, and how much is just running in the background.

To do that, I want to look at what the researchers actually found, how our brains build these automatic routines in the first place, why some of them quietly work in our favor while others don’t, and what the research suggests about changing the habits we’d rather not have.

What the Researchers Did

In 2025, a team led by Amanda Rebar at the University of South Carolina, along with Benjamin Gardner at the University of Surrey and Grace Vincent at Central Queensland University, set out to answer a deceptively simple question: how much of what we do each day is actually driven by habit?

To find out, they recruited 105 people in the UK and Australia and asked them to carry a simple task for one week. Six times a day, at random intervals, each person’s phone would buzz with a short survey. The questions were straightforward: what are you doing right now, and did you choose to do it, or did it just happen?

The activities people reported were ordinary. Work tasks, household chores, looking at a screen, eating, drinking. Exercise showed up too, but far less often, accounting for only about 6% of the recorded activities.

When the researchers analyzed the responses, they found that roughly two thirds of daily behaviors were initiated by habit. People weren’t sitting down and weighing up whether to do these things. The behavior just started, triggered by familiar cues in familiar settings.

Earlier estimates had put the figure lower. In a widely cited 2002 study, psychologist Wendy Wood and her colleagues at Duke University found that around 43% of daily behaviors were repeated in the same context while people were thinking about something else. The new study pushed the number significantly higher, largely because it measured something more specific: not just whether a behavior was repeated, but whether it was started without any deliberate choice at all.

What made the finding more interesting was the relationship between habit and intention. Nearly half of all recorded behaviors were both habitual and intentional. In other words, people had at some point chosen to do these things, and over time the choice had become automatic. As the researchers put it, switching from cereal to overnight oats might take effort on day one, but after a few weeks it’s just what you do.

The one exception was exercise. People often started a workout out of habit, heading to the gym at the usual time without really thinking about it. But unlike most other habits, the workout itself still required conscious effort to see through. Starting was automatic. Finishing wasn’t.

The full study was published in Psychology & Health in 2025.

How Autopilot Gets Built

Once a behavior becomes habitual, it feels effortless. But it doesn’t start that way.

Think about the first time you drove a car. Every action needed your full attention. Checking the mirrors, pressing the clutch at the right moment, judging the distance to the car in front. Now think about your last drive to work or the store. You probably arrived without remembering most of the journey.

That’s basically how habits form. When we repeat a behavior in a consistent setting, the brain gradually learns to associate the context with the action. Over time, the situation itself becomes the trigger. You don’t decide to reach for your seatbelt when you get in the car. The act of sitting down does it for you.

In a great TEDx talk exploring the science behind this process, Harvard researcher Marco Badwal walks through how repeated behaviors physically reshape the brain’s wiring, and why so much of what we do each day runs without our conscious involvement.

What Badwal describes at the neural level has been studied more formally in terms of timing. In 2009, a research team led by Phillippa Lally at University College London asked 96 volunteers to adopt a new daily behavior, something as simple as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or going for a short walk after dinner, and tracked how long it took for the behavior to start feeling automatic. The average was 66 days, but the range was striking. For some people it took as few as 18 days. For others, more than 250. The popular claim that it takes 21 days to build a habit doesn’t hold up against the evidence.

What’s happening underneath is a gradual handoff inside the brain. When a behavior is new, the prefrontal cortex does the heavy lifting. That’s the part of the brain involved in planning and decision making. But as the behavior is repeated in the same context, the basal ganglia, a deeper structure involved in pattern recognition and routine, begins to take over. The action no longer needs a deliberate decision. It runs on its own.

This isn’t a flaw. It’s how the brain avoids running out of steam. If every small action required the same level of attention as a new one, we’d be overwhelmed before lunchtime. In Japanese martial arts and traditional crafts, this process has a name. Kata refers to forms that are practiced so often they become second nature. The aim isn’t mindless repetition. It’s to free the conscious mind for higher-level awareness, precisely because the fundamentals no longer need thinking about.

When Autopilot Helps

Having your day run largely on autopilot is one of the brain’s most useful tricks.

Consider what your morning would look like without habits. You’d have to think about how to hold your toothbrush, which order to get dressed in, how much coffee to put in the machine. Every small decision would demand the same attention as a new one. By the time you left the house, you’d already be mentally drained, and you wouldn’t have done anything meaningful yet.

Habits take care of that. They handle the routine so your conscious mind can focus on things that actually need thinking about. It’s the reason you can cook a meal you’ve made dozens of times while carrying on a conversation.

Rebar and her colleagues found that many of these automatic behaviors weren’t working against people’s wishes. They were routines that lined up with what people actually wanted to be doing. The breakfast example the researchers used is a good one. Switching what you eat in the morning might take effort on day one, but once the habit forms, the healthier choice happens on its own.

Wood’s earlier research pointed to a similar finding. In her 2002 study, habitual behaviors were associated with lower levels of reported stress than non-habitual ones. That makes sense. When a behavior is automatic, there’s no internal negotiation, no weighing up of options, no willpower required. It just happens. And that efficiency, repeated across dozens of small actions every day, adds up.

As Gardner put it:

Good habits may be a powerful way to make our goals a reality.

When Autopilot Works Against Us

But not all habits are working in our favor.

The same system that gets you through your day without thinking is also the one behind the less helpful patterns. Picking up your phone without any real reason to. Reaching for a snack you weren’t hungry for. Staying up later than you planned because the scrolling just kept going.

The habit doesn’t check whether you still want to do it. It just fires. A cue appears, the routine runs, and by the time you’re aware of what’s happening, you’re already halfway through it. That’s fine when the habit is brushing your teeth. It’s less fine when it’s something you’ve been meaning to stop.

This is where the common advice to “just try harder” runs into trouble. Willpower operates in the conscious mind. Habits don’t. They run underneath, triggered by context before any decision gets made. Trying to override a habit with willpower is a bit like trying to steer a car by shouting at it. You might get a result occasionally, but you’re fighting the wrong mechanism.

Gardner was direct about this:

For people who want to break their bad habits, simply telling them to ‘try harder’ isn’t enough. To create lasting change, we must incorporate strategies to help people recognize and disrupt their unwanted habits, and ideally form positive new ones in their place.

There’s an important distinction here, too. Some habits aren’t harmful in themselves. They’ve just gone stale. A routine that made sense in one phase of life can quietly persist into another where it no longer fits. You don’t always notice, because the whole point of a habit is that you don’t have to think about it.

What Helps (and What Doesn’t)

If willpower isn’t the answer, what is?

The research team were fairly clear on this. Lasting change isn’t about finding more motivation. It’s about setting up the right conditions for a new behavior to take root and repeat.

One of the most practical strategies is linking a new behavior to a specific, predictable moment in the day. Rather than telling yourself you’ll exercise “more often,” you connect it to something that already happens reliably. After I finish work. Before I eat dinner. The existing routine becomes the cue, and over time the new behavior gets pulled along with it.

This isn’t a new idea. Lally’s 2009 research showed that the people who successfully formed new habits were the ones who repeated the behavior in the same context, consistently. It wasn’t the difficulty of the behavior that mattered most. It was the consistency of the setting.

Breaking unwanted habits works along similar lines, but in reverse. Instead of building a new cue, you disrupt an existing one. If you always reach for your phone first thing in the morning, charging it in a different room changes the context enough to interrupt the pattern. If a particular environment triggers a behavior you’re trying to stop, changing the environment is often more effective than trying to resist the urge once it’s already started.

What both approaches have in common is that they work with the system rather than against it. They don’t ask you to overpower a habit through sheer force of will. They change the conditions that trigger it in the first place.

Grace Vincent, one of the researchers behind the 2025 study, summarized this well:

If we set out to create a positive habit, whether that’s around better sleep hygiene, or nutrition, or general wellbeing improvements, we can rely on an internal ‘autopilot’ to take over and help us maintain those habits.

The irony is worth noting. The same autopilot that can keep an unhelpful pattern running for years is also the thing that makes a better pattern stick, once it’s been given the chance to form.

Final Thoughts

If this research finding proves to be robust, and it really is the case that roughly two thirds of what we do each day happens without a conscious decision, it raises an interesting question. Not whether that’s a good or bad thing, but whether the habits running our day are still the ones we’d choose if we were starting from scratch.

Most of our habits are ones we built on purpose, even if we don’t remember building them. They started as choices, and through repetition they became automatic. That’s not a loss of control. In many cases, it’s the opposite. It’s the brain honoring a decision we made and carrying it forward so we don’t have to keep making it.

Some of those habits will be exactly where you want them. Others might have quietly drifted out of step with who you are now.

If nothing else, the research is an invitation to notice. Not to overhaul everything, but to pay attention for a day and see what comes up. You might be surprised by how much of it you’d keep.

If you stopped at a few points during your day and asked yourself, did I choose to do this, or did it just happen, what do you think you’d find? I’d love to hear your thoughts in the comments.
