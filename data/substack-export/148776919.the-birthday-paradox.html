Here’s a quick exercise you can try with your friends and family.

Ask them to think about a group of people. This could be a social media group, the fans of a sports team they follow, or a group of people at work or school.

Then, ask them how many people they think would need to be in that group for it to be more likely than not (i.e., more than a 50% chance) that two members share the same birthday.

Once they give their answer, tell them that, surprisingly, you only need a group of 23 people for there to be a greater than 50% chance that two members share the same birthday.

Most people will find this hard to believe and will estimate a much higher number than 23 for this birthday match to occur.

This seemingly counterintuitive result is known as the birthday paradox, or the birthday problem. It’s a great way to get people thinking about randomness, probability, and chance.

If you and your friends and family would like to learn more about the birthday paradox, I recommend this excellent and humorous BBC podcast, where the hosts, along with mathematician Alex Bellos, discuss the idea of randomness. Start at 15:31 to hear the conversation on the birthday paradox.

As Alex Bellos notes, “Most coincidences, if you actually crunch the numbers, become a lot less amazing.”

Another quick and easy way to explore the birthday paradox is to find the birthdates of a group of around 30 people and check for any matching dates of birth. For example, I looked at the cast list of The Walking Dead and discovered that Tom Payne (who played Jesus) and Steven Yeun (who played Glenn) both celebrate their birthdays on December 21st.

What are the odds? A lot more likely than you’d think. Just don’t tell Han Solo!
