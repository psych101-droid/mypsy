One Halloween night at a famously haunted sanatorium-turned-attraction, a woman known only as S.M. did something unbelievable: while other visitors screamed in response to the continuous jump scares, S.M. laughed and even poked at the “monsters” that leapt out at her. On one very serious occasion, a man held a knife to her throat and threatened to cut her. Yet S.M. felt no panic at all.

Dangerous snakes and spiders don’t bother her in the slightest. She can watch the scariest movies with benign indifference, observing that while others might be terrified, she herself feels nothing.

In short, S.M. is a woman without fear, an ordinary mother of three in many ways, except for the extraordinary fact that fear is a feeling completely foreign to her.

The Rare Disease That Destroyed Her Fear

S.M.’s fearless existence is caused by a rare genetic brain disorder. In her late childhood, S.M. developed Urbach–Wiethe disease, an extremely rare condition (only a few hundred cases have been documented worldwide). This disease caused calcium deposits to build up in S.M.’s medial temporal lobes, slowly destroying her amygdalae (two almond-shaped clusters deep in the brain that are crucial for processing fear).

Brain scans show nothing but “black holes” where her left and right amygdalae should be. Without functioning amygdalae, the brain’s typical fear center is effectively gone, and the signals that normally flood our body during fear (the racing heart, the sweating palms) never get triggered in S.M.

Remarkably, aside from this isolated neurological anomaly, S.M. is completely normal. Her intelligence, memory, and other emotions (joy, sadness, anger, and so on) are intact. She laughs, cries, and gets angry like anyone else. But ask her what it’s like to feel afraid, and she’ll admit she truly has “no clue.”

For scientists Antonio Damasio, Daniel Tranel, and Ralph Adolphs (who first began working with S.M. in the early 1990s), this rare case was a golden opportunity. Here was someone who had lived most of her life without fear, offering a unique window into the brain’s fear system.

Over decades of study, S.M. has helped researchers confirm the central role of the amygdala in generating fear and reading fearful cues. For example, early experiments showed that S.M. has great difficulty recognizing when others look afraid. She can interpret most facial expressions, but not fear in others’ faces. In one study, she was even unable to draw a picture of a “scared” face, pausing with pencil in hand because she didn’t know what a fearful expression should look like.

Such findings have helped cement the amygdala’s reputation as a key hub for fear processing and emotional learning.

Life in a World With No Fear

By all accounts, S.M.’s day-to-day life is both fascinating and perilous. On one hand, she describes feeling curiosity and excitement in situations that would terrify most people. During the haunted house visit, for instance, she compared her emotions to the thrill of a rollercoaster: exciting, but never scary.

In one study, researchers gave S.M. a handheld diary-like device to record her emotions at random times over the course of several months. She reported feeling the full range of everyday emotions, including happiness, anger, surprise, and even sadness. Everything, that is, except fear. In fact, the word she selected most often to describe herself was "fearless," topping the list of 50 possible emotions over a three-month period.

S.M. is often described as extremely outgoing, friendly, and uninhibited. She approaches strangers with an ease and warmth that most of us would find incredibly difficult to emulate. She has even been noted to be playfully flirtatious and unusually trusting, seemingly unbothered by the social caution that keeps others wary of new people.

It seems as though the anxiety or suspicion that typically holds people back simply doesn’t exist for her. S.M. has an abnormally high desire to approach others, coupled with an impaired ability to recognize negative social cues that would typically signal someone to keep their distance. For example, she doesn’t pick up on subtle signs of fear or threat in someone’s tone of voice or facial expression.

She also has no sense of personal space. She can stand nose to nose with someone and stare into their eyes without discomfort, whereas most people would feel anxious or threatened in that situation. In many ways, her fearless brain makes her exceptionally open and socially disinhibited, which can be both a gift and a risk.

The Hidden Dangers of Feeling No Fear

While never feeling fear might sound liberating, S.M.’s story also reveals how essential fear is for keeping us safe. Lacking any kind of inner alarm bell, S.M. has a troubling history of walking straight into danger. As mentioned above, In one especially harrowing incident, a man in a park grabbed her, held a knife to her throat, and snarled, “I’m going to cut you!” Most of us would be paralyzed by terror in that moment, but S.M. felt only annoyance. She replied coolly, “Go ahead and cut me. I’ll be coming back, and I’ll haunt your ass,” showing no hint of panic. Stunned, the attacker released her, and S.M. simply walked away. She even returned to the same park the very next day.

Without fear to urge caution, she doesn’t avoid the dark alleys, sketchy parks, or toxic people that the rest of us instinctively steer clear of. As a result, trouble finds her far more often than it would someone with an intact fear system. Essentially, she is blind to the danger cues that normally scream “Run away!” or “Stay out of there!” These are the very cues that fear evolved to teach us.

Being biologically fearless has made S.M. uniquely vulnerable. Those studying her case have kept her identity strictly confidential for decades, precisely because a person with no fear can be easily exploited by ill-intentioned individuals. She might trust the wrong person or fail to recognize when someone means her harm. Fear is a lifesaving gift of evolution. S.M. lacks that gift, to her detriment, mostly!

Ironically, S.M.’s lack of fear shields her from psychological harm after the fact. She has survived terrifying ordeals, yet because she did not feel terror at the time, those events left no trauma scar on her psyche. In her own view, these weren’t even “bad events.” In other words, without fear, trauma isn’t really traumatizing.

This remarkably suggests that it’s the emotional fear response during an event that helps encode it as a traumatic memory. S.M.’s case supports what research on emotion and memory has long shown. Typically, fear activates the amygdala to stamp frightening events into our memory banks, often too strongly, as in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Take away the amygdala and the fear, and even a near-death experience might be remembered as calmly as a trip to the grocery store.

S.M. herself remains upbeat and positive, despite objectively experiencing more than her share of violence and adversity. She simply doesn’t dwell on those moments or develop anxiety about them the way we might. It’s an astonishing trade-off. S.M. is free from fear and free from fear’s psychological aftermath, but that very freedom puts her in harm’s way.

When Terror Comes from Within

It was widely thought that S.M. was literally incapable of fear under any circumstance. But in 2013, a team of researchers finally discovered one thing that did scare S.M.

When S.M. volunteered for an experiment in which she inhaled a mixture of air enriched with 35% carbon dioxide (a concentration that creates an overwhelming sensation of suffocation), the result was startling. Within seconds of taking a single breath of the CO₂-rich air, S.M. was overcome by a wave of panic. Her eyes bulged, her heart pounded, and she frantically clawed at the oxygen mask, gasping for air.

S.M. and two other patients with identical amygdala damage all reacted the same way: with pure terror. This sudden surge of fear was something none of them had ever experienced before.

Why would an odorless, invisible gas trigger fear in a woman who didn’t fear snakes or armed attackers? The answer reveals a crucial insight into how our brain processes threats. The carbon dioxide induced a feeling of internal suffocation, an internal threat, which the body monitors through a different pathway than it uses for external dangers.

Normally, when we see or hear an external threat (like a growling dog or a man with a knife), that information is relayed to the amygdala, which in turn fires up the brain’s “panic circuit” and initiates the classic fight-or-flight response. But the suffocation alarm doesn’t rely on the amygdala at all. Instead, specialized sensors in the body detect high CO₂ levels and send distress signals directly to the brainstem and other regions (sometimes called the “reptilian” parts of the brain).

In S.M.’s case, those brainstem circuits were perfectly intact, her only damage was to the amygdala. So when the CO₂ hit her system, it bypassed the missing fear center and still managed to trigger full-blown panic via an alternate route.

In essence, the experiment uncovered a backdoor pathway to fear and in doing so, showed that the human brain has multiple, redundant alarm systems to protect us. If one fails, another can sound the alarm.

This finding was a eureka moment in neuroscience. It refined our understanding of fear from the simplistic view that “all fear starts in the amygdala” to a more nuanced perspective consisting of a whole fear network, involving the amygdala and other brain systems working in concert.

What S.M. Teaches Us About Fear

S.M.’s extraordinary story is far more than a medical curiosity, it’s a powerful lens on why fear exists and how it shapes our lives. Fear is often seen as a negative feeling, something we all wish we felt less of. But evolutionary psychology reminds us that fear is an ancient survival mechanism, honed over millions of years to protect us from harm.

Without fear, our early human ancestors would have strolled up to predators or stayed lazily in a burning forest. The ones who felt fear, ran away, and lived to see another day and passed on their genes; we are all, therefore, descendants of the careful, the wary, and the easily spooked.

Fear lives in each of us as a primal guardian. S.M.’s predicament brilliantly illustrates this: without fear, she lacks a critical survival tool the rest of us take for granted. It’s not hard to imagine that if S.M. had lived in a less modern environment (full of real predators or dangers requiring split-second fight-or-flight responses), her story might have been much shorter.

Yet, S.M.’s case also challenges us to think about fear in our modern lives. Today, many of us live in relatively safe environments, and our fear systems are sometimes triggered by threats that aren’t truly life-or-death, like public speaking, or a rustle in the dark that turns out to be the wind. We often experience “false alarms” or excessive anxiety because our brains are still wired for lions and snakes.

The fact that despite all the danger she’s encountered, S.M. remains overwhelmingly positive and free from the emotional burdens fear can impose on the mind, hints at the psychological cost of fear, namely, how fear can often hold us back unnecessarily.

Of course, for most of us fear is not the enemy; it’s a teacher and protector. Healthy fear keeps pushes us to flee from a dangerous situation. We can be grateful that our brains sound the alarm when they should. But perhaps we can also take a page from S.M.’s book by appreciating the moments when fearlessness has its advantages, like being open and kind to others without bias.

S.M.’s life without fear shows both sides: the virtues of fearlessness and the perils of living without fear’s guidance. By studying S.M., scientists have deepened their understanding of panic attacks, phobias, PTSD, and the general neurobiology of fear and emotion. Her case underscores that fear is not just a vague feeling but a tangible biological function, one that can be broken or lost, with dramatic effects.

In the end, the incredible case of S.M. is as much a human story as a scientific one. It invites us to imagine life with our fear circuit switched off; the thrills we might seek, the dangers we might overlook, and the precious value of fear in our own lives.

So the next time your heart pounds and your palms sweat at something scary, you might remember S.M. and reflect on the strange gift that is fear: unpleasant yet invaluable, a sensation that has helped keep you alive to feel anything at all.
