The Birth of Personality Assessment

On December 31, 1691, German Enlightenment philosopher and jurist Christian Thomasius published one of the earliest accounts of systematic personality assessment. Presented as a letter with the delightfully elaborate title, "New Discovery of a Solid Science, Most Necessary for the Community, for Discerning the Secrets of the Heart of Other Men from Daily Conversation, Even Against Their Will," this groundbreaking work laid the foundation for understanding individual differences in personality.

Thomasius proposed that the key to uncovering a person’s true nature lies in distinguishing between their natural and affected feelings, as well as recognizing that people often resist displaying raw, unfiltered emotions. This early insight into human behavior marked a significant step toward the systematic study of personality.

Building on his initial work, Thomasius later introduced the first quantitative psychological rating scale based on four core human inclinations:

  1. Rational Love (Vernünftige Liebe)

  2. Sensuousness (Wollust)

  3. Ambition (Ehrgeiz)

  4. Acquisitiveness (Geldgeiz)

Thomasius believed that while everyone possesses these inclinations to varying degrees, one will always dominate. To measure these individual differences, he devised a scoring system: the dominant inclination received 60 points, the weakest inclination a minimum of 5 points, and the two remaining inclinations were scored by calculating the difference between the strongest and weakest scores.

This innovative method provided an early glimpse into the systematic quantification of personality traits—a cornerstone of modern psychological assessment. Thomasius’s work not only anticipated future explorations of human behavior but also exemplified the Enlightenment spirit of applying reason to uncover the mysteries of the human condition.

The ultimate goal of personality psychology is to construct a scientifically credible account of psychological individuality. Such an account must situate a person in a biological and cultural context while specifying how that person is similar to and different from other persons.

Psychology Classic

What do animated geometric shapes reveal about how we perceive human intentions, emotions, and motives? The landmark study An Experimental Study of Apparent Behavior by Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel is a groundbreaking exploration of interpersonal perception and the attribution process.

In this fascinating research, participants watched a short film featuring simple geometric shapes—triangles and circles—moving in seemingly random patterns. Yet, participants couldn’t help but attribute human-like motivations and emotions to these shapes: a large triangle became a “bully,” a small triangle a “hero,” and a circle a “victim.” This experiment illuminated the human tendency to perceive agency, intention, and emotion in even the simplest visual stimuli.

I have a terrible memory for remembering names. Is there anything I can do to improve this using tips and research findings from psychology?

Insights from cognitive psychology by: Jess Anderson

Here are some practical tips and insights from cognitive psychology to help improve your memory for remembering names:

Pay Attention and Be Present: One of the most crucial factors in remembering names is to pay close attention when you're introduced to someone. Often, forgetting a name happens because we're not fully present during the introduction. Make a conscious effort to focus on the person and their name.

Repeat and Reinforce: After you've been introduced, use the person's name in the conversation. For example, "Nice to meet you, Sarah." This repetition helps reinforce the memory trace. Repetition is a powerful tool for memory enhancement.

Use Visual Imagery: Try to create a mental image that connects the person's name with a distinctive visual cue. For example, if you meet someone named "Rose," imagine them holding a rose. Associating a name with a visual image can make it easier to recall.

Association: Connect the person's name with something you already know or a familiar person. For example, if the person has the same name as a friend or celebrity, associate the two in your mind.

Use the Name Immediately: In conversations, try to use the person's name naturally. "What do you think, David?" Using the name shortly after learning it helps reinforce memory.

Mindful Repetition: Spend a few minutes at the end of the day recalling the names of people you met. This mindful repetition can solidify memory storage.

Contextualize: If you meet someone in a specific context (at a party, in a class, at work), mentally associate their name with that context. This can provide a helpful retrieval cue later.

Active Listening: When people introduce themselves, they may also share something about themselves. Pay attention to these details as they can help you remember their names later.

Mnemonic Devices: Create mnemonic devices or memory aids to help remember names. These can be rhymes, alliterations, or acronyms based on the person's name.

Practice and Patience: Improving memory takes practice. Be patient with yourself and consistently apply these techniques.

Remember that memory improvement is a gradual process, and not all techniques may work equally well for everyone. Experiment with these strategies to find what works best for you. Over time, with consistent effort, you'll likely notice improvement in your ability to remember names.
