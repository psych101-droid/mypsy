I recently came across an essay published in The Atlantic Monthly in February 1863. Its title was "The Utility and the Futility of Aphorisms," and its author was the American writer and clergyman William R. Alger.

An aphorism is a concise statement claiming a truth of general importance. What distinguishes it is its depth, its precision, and sometimes paradoxical quality that makes you look twice. It doesn’t simply state a fact or prescribe a course of action. It makes you pause, reflect, and think more deeply.

As someone who's always been drawn to aphorisms Alger’s article immediately caught my attention. Alger wasn't simply celebrating the wisdom of short, seemingly insightful sayings. He was interrogating them, asking when they genuinely help us think and act, and when they do the opposite. According to Alger:

The best aphorisms are pointed expressions of the results of observation, experience, and reflection. They are portable wisdom, the quintessential extracts of thought and feeling. They furnish the largest amount of intellectual stimulus and nutriment in the smallest compass.

For example, Friedrich Nietzsche’s famous aphorism “That which does not kill us makes us stronger.”

Alger’s case for the aphorism is made with real conviction but he also contends that while they can open the mind, they can also close it down, if they function as ready-made phrases that spare us the effort of thinking for ourselves.

Before exploring what contemporary psychology has to say about the utility and futility of aphorisms, it's worth identifying the related terms that are often used interchangeably but mean something quite different.

Not all sayings are created equal

A proverb is different in origin and character. Proverbs emerge from folk culture and collective experience. They’re traditional, often metaphorical, and have been handed down across generations. Nobody wrote them; they accumulated. “A stitch in time saves nine” is a proverb. “Don’t count your chickens before they hatch” is a proverb. As Alger observed, they’re the children of anonymous observation, rising from among the multitude rather than from a single named mind.

A maxim is more individual and more directive. Where a proverb describes, a maxim prescribes. It’s a rule for conduct, a guide to behavior. Maxims typically tell you what to do. They’re less interested in opening questions than in settling them. Check out the following video for some great maxims.

An adage sits close to the proverb but carries the additional weight of age. It’s a saying that has gained credibility not through any particular insight but through sheer longevity, the accumulated authority of having been repeated across centuries. “Least said, soonest mended” is an adage. Its authority is essentially traditional.

An epigram is wit compressed into a line. It aims for surprise, irony, or satirical effect rather than wisdom in any deep sense. It delights rather than instructs. Oscar Wilde’s “I can resist everything except temptation” is just one of his many memorable epigrams.

An axiom is a self-evident premise, the kind of foundational statement used in logic or mathematics. “The shortest distance between two points is a straight line” is an axiom. It doesn’t invite reflection; it closes inquiry.

A truism is a statement so obviously true that saying it adds nothing. It has the shape of wisdom without the substance.

A dictum is an authoritative pronouncement associated with a specific source, a ruling, a declaration, a verdict. It carries weight through the status of whoever issued it rather than through any intrinsic insight.

In his 2012 book The Long and Short of It, Professor Gary Saul Morson argues that what separates a genuine aphorism from these related terms, is that the true aphorism addresses the perplexing nature of the world and invites deeper questioning.

Which brings us back to Nietzsche’s “What does not kill me makes me stronger.” By Morson’s definition the quote earns its place as a genuine aphorism. It doesn’t simply prescribe conduct the way a maxim does. It makes a claim about the nature of adversity that we can test against our own experience, which personally I have found partially true, partially complicated, and never quite fully resolved. It invites further thought rather than closing down the issue raised. That’s the mark of the aphorism.

Interestingly, “What does not kill me makes me stronger” comes from a section of Nietzsche’s book Twilight of the Idols that he titled “Maxims and Arrows” suggesting that the boundaries between the terms outlined above are not always clear cut.

Even scholars who study them professionally disagree about where one ends and another begins. Philosopher Daniel Dennett coined the term “deepity” to describe a statement that appears to straddle the line between profound and empty: it has two possible readings, one trivially true, one that sounds profound but is actually meaningless. “Love is just a word” is his example. The deepity mimics the aphorism’s compression and apparent depth while delivering neither.

How aphorisms work on the brain

The reason aphorisms resonate when we encounter them, sometimes has more to do with the way our brains process information than the insight they contain.

The term processing fluency describes the ease with which the mind takes in and makes sense of information. Research has established that when something is easy to process, whether because it’s brief, rhythmically regular, or familiar, the brain experiences a low-level sense of comfort. However, that feeling of ease can be misattributed to the quality of the information itself. In other words, if something feels easy to absorb, we’re inclined to treat it as more likely to be true. This is known as the illusory truth effect.

A 1999 study by Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz illustrated this by manipulating something as simple as the visual contrast of text on a screen. Statements displayed in higher contrast, and therefore easier to read, were rated by participants as more likely to be true than identical statements displayed in lower contrast. The content hadn’t changed. Only the ease of reading had. Yet that ease was enough to shift judgments of truth.

For aphorisms specifically, a clear expression of this phenomenon can be seen in what is known as the rhyme-as-reason effect. In a study published in Psychological Science in 2000, Matthew McGlone and Jessica Tofighbakhsh presented participants with aphorisms in their original rhyming form alongside semantically equivalent versions with the rhyme removed. “What sobriety conceals, alcohol reveals” was tested against “What sobriety conceals, alcohol unmasks.” The rhyming versions were consistently judged to be more accurate, not because they contained better evidence or sharper reasoning, but because the structural regularity of the rhyme made them easier to process. The researchers described the tendency to judge a statement’s truth by its aesthetic qualities as the Keats heuristic, after the poet’s line “Beauty is truth, truth beauty.”

The finding has a direct bearing on why aphorisms carry such conviction. A well-constructed aphorism doesn’t just state something thought-provoking; it states it in a way that feels right. The rhythm, the compression, the satisfying click of the phrasing all work on us before we’ve had a chance to evaluate the content. As Alger observed in 1863, the aphorism hits some fact of experience with a pungency that fixes it in the mind. What he couldn’t have known is that part of what fixes it there is a cognitive mechanism that has nothing to do with whether the observation is actually correct.

There is, however, a redeeming detail in McGlone and Tofighbakhsh’s findings. When participants were explicitly cautioned to separate a statement’s poetic qualities from its meaning, the truth advantage of the rhyming versions was significantly reduced. Awareness, it turns out, is a partial antidote. Which matters, because as Alger himself argued, the real danger of aphorisms isn’t that they’re wrong. It’s that they can feel so right that we never think to check.

Aphorisms and the question of utility

One tentative area the evidence points to is clinical practice. A 2023 review published in the American Journal of Psychotherapy by psychiatrists Joel Yager and Jerry Kay titled “Adages, Aphorisms, and Proverbs in Psychiatry and Psychotherapy: Tools for Assessment and Treatment” examined the use of aphorisms, subsumed within the broader term adage, which they identified as heuristic cognitive structures, shortcuts that help patients assess difficult situations, regulate difficult emotions, and find frameworks for action when thinking clearly is hardest. These structures were found to be located within five broad categories: those that encouraged positive reinterpretation, those that addressed destiny and hardship, those that built resilience, those that justify through experience, and those that offer relational insight.

It’s an appealing framework, and clinically it makes intuitive sense. When someone is in the grip of acute distress, a well-chosen pearl of wisdom can offer a handhold, something brief enough to hold in mind when more elaborate reasoning isn’t available. But Yager and Kay are honest about the limits of what the evidence can currently claim. As they note, the extent to which well-known phrases that spontaneously arise in psychiatric and psychotherapeutic interactions, and what effect they have when they do, hasn’t been systematically studied. The clinical case rests on narrative review, clinical observation, and case examples rather than controlled trial data.

A more specific clinical example comes from psychotherapist Richard O’Connor, whose 2001 paper in the American Journal of Psychotherapy describes using a dedicated list of aphorisms as a deliberate therapeutic tool with depressed patients. In his own words: “I often provide them with a list of aphorisms about depression, which can serve as a stimulus for thought and discussion. I find that the following flat assertions, presented as statements of fact, have a way of getting around defensiveness.” O’Connor describes these aphorisms as a form of what the clinician Leston Havens called performative language, statements that perform an action simply by being spoken, using them to move patients from a passive position toward active engagement with their condition. As with Yager and Kay, this is practitioner evidence evidence rather than controlled trial data, but it offers a concrete illustration of aphorisms being used purposefully, with a named rationale, in a peer-reviewed context.

There’s a separate and more firmly established reason why aphorisms may serve us well, and it has to do with memory. In a study published in Psychonomic Science in 1969, Gordon Bower and his graduate student Michal Clark demonstrated that participants who constructed stories to link lists of unrelated words recalled six to seven times more words than participants who used no such structure, with median recall of 93% against 13% after all twelve lists had been studied. Aphorisms aren’t stories, but they share something important with them: a structural shape, a beginning and an end, a form that gives the mind something to hold. That structural quality may be part of what makes an aphorism easier to retain and return to than a more discursive observation of equal insight. When we need a thought most, in a moment of difficulty or decision, it helps if that thought is already there.

When aphorisms shut thinking down

In a narrative essay published in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 2023, physician Lisa Schreyer wrote about the psychological cost of following a childhood therapist’s advice to “fake it till you make it.” Schreyer’s is a personal account rather than empirical research, and it would be wrong to draw broad conclusions from a single experience. It’s also worth noting that “fake it till you make it” sits closer to a maxim than a genuine aphorism by the definitions we’ve established: it prescribes a course of action rather than inviting deeper reflection on the nature of things. But that distinction doesn’t diminish the point it illustrates. Whether the saying in question is an aphorism, a maxim, or something in between, the psychological cost of following the wrong one without questioning it is real. And that cost is what the concept of the thought-terminating cliché was developed to explain.

The most direct theoretical framework for understanding this comes from an unlikely source. In his 1961 book Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism, the psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton introduced the concept of the thought-terminating cliché, which he explained as follows:

The most far-reaching and complex of human problems are compressed into brief, highly reductive, definitive-sounding phrases, easily memorized and easily expressed. These become the start and finish of any ideological analysis.

Lifton coined the term specifically in the context of totalitarianism and applying it to everyday aphorisms extends beyond its original scope. A thought-terminating cliché in Lifton’s sense was a tool of ideological control, deployed deliberately to discourage independent thinking in a coercive environment. Most aphorisms we encounter in daily life carry no such intent.

Dr Catriona Davis-McCabe, former President of the Australian Psychological Society, notes that thought-terminating clichés can be used to shut down discussion and debate across a range of settings, from organisations and workplaces to families and relationships. Her practical advice, therefore, is to examine the clichés you reach for, question where they come from, and ask whether they are genuinely serving you or simply closing down thinking that deserves to happen

What’s striking is that Alger identified the same dangers specifically in relation to aphorisms in 1863.

The first danger, then, from aphorisms is that they may enable us to evade, instead of helping us to fulfil, the duty of meeting and solving for ourselves each mental exigency as it arises.

He also identified a second danger: that aphorisms may be applied mechanically, without genuine understanding, leading to grievous mistakes. His warning wasn’t about totalitarianism. It was about the more ordinary human tendency to reach for a ready phrase and mistake the comfort it provides for the work of actually thinking.

Final Thoughts

So do Alger’s arguments still hold?

His case for the utility of aphorisms finds support in the research on memory and structure, in the clinical evidence that expressions of compressed wisdom can offer a useful of reference in moments of acute distress, and in the work of practitioners who have deployed them deliberately as therapeutic tools. This doesn’t prove that aphorisms heal, but it does suggest why they can help.

Alger’s case for the futility of aphorisms also finds support. The rhyme-as-reason effect demonstrates that we judge the truth of a statement partly by the aesthetic pleasure of its form, not just its content. Processing fluency shows that ease of absorption can be mistaken for evidence of truth. And Lifton’s thought-terminating cliché framework, however far removed from its original context, names precisely the mechanism Alger was warning against: the way a well-turned phrase can become the start and finish of analysis rather than a prompt to deeper thought.

Alger didn’t have an explanation for why this is so hard to resist. The comfort that a good aphorism provides isn’t incidental to its danger. It’s the source of it. When something feels right, the impulse to interrogate it further is diminished. McGlone and Tofighbakhsh’s finding that awareness attenuates the rhyme-as-reason effect suggests that the antidote, partial as it is, lies in the same faculty Alger was appealing to all along: the willingness to keep thinking.

Alger closed his 1863 essay with a distinction that psychology has since reframed in cognitive terms. There are, he argued, two kinds of verbal wisdom. One is what he called a petrification of wisdom: lifeless, dried out, a husk of insight that neither stimulates nor nourishes. The other is a vital concentration of wisdom: alive, charged, capable of genuine transmission. The same words, the same form, the same compression. The difference lies not in the aphorism itself but in what the reader brings to it.

An aphorism is not wise or foolish by nature. It’s an invitation. Whether it opens thinking or closes it down depends, as it always has, on whether we accept the invitation or simply take the comfort and move on.

Accepting the invitation

Here are four classic aphorisms that I think genuinely meet Morson’s standard: concise statements that address the perplexing nature of the world and invite deeper questioning rather than closing it down. See what you make of them.

Hippocrates
