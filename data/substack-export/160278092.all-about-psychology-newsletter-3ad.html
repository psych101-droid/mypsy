Abraham Maslow

Today in the history of psychology, April 1, 1908, Abraham Maslow was born in Brooklyn, New York, to Russian-Jewish immigrant parents. Despite a challenging upbringing marked by poverty and discrimination, Maslow's intellectual curiosity led him to pursue higher education at the City College of New York and later the University of Wisconsin, where he earned his doctorate in psychology.​

In the mid-20th century, Maslow emerged as a pioneering figure in psychology, challenging the prevailing dominance of psychoanalysis and behaviorism. He introduced humanistic psychology, often referred to as the "third force," emphasizing the inherent goodness and potential of human beings. Central to his theory was the hierarchy of needs, a motivational model outlining a progression from basic physiological necessities to higher-order psychological aspirations, culminating in self-actualization—the fulfillment of one's unique potential and capabilities.​

Beyond self-actualization, Maslow explored profound moments of heightened awareness and fulfillment, which he termed peak experiences. These are instances where individuals feel a deep sense of connection, euphoria, and understanding, often accompanied by a perception of transcending ordinary consciousness. Maslow believed such experiences were integral to personal growth and could provide profound insights into human potential. ​

Maslow's contributions extended beyond theoretical frameworks. In 1968, he was elected president of the American Psychological Association (APA), underscoring his influence and the widespread recognition of his work within the psychological community. His legacy endures in various fields, including education, management, and psychotherapy, where his insights into human motivation and personal development continue to inspire and guide.​

As we commemorate the anniversary of Maslow's birth, we honor a visionary who illuminated pathways to understanding the complexities of human nature, encouraging us to strive for growth, fulfillment, and the realization of our highest potentials.

Why do I get so impatient, even over small things—and what can I do to stay calm when everything feels too slow?

Impatience is something we’ve all felt—waiting in a slow-moving queue, dealing with a buffering video, or getting irritated when someone talks too slowly. But behind that quick burst of frustration lies something deeper: impatience is a psychological signal—a flare sent up by the brain when your expectations of time, control, or reward aren’t being met.

At its core, impatience is a tension between desire and delay. Your brain craves efficiency, closure, and forward motion. When it perceives that progress is too slow or blocked altogether, it generates an emotional response: irritation, restlessness, and that all-too-familiar internal "hurry up!" soundtrack.

This reaction is especially strong in modern life, where technology has conditioned us for instant gratification. We expect food in minutes, answers in seconds, and outcomes without delay. The gap between wanting and waiting has shrunk dramatically—and our tolerance for slowness has shrunk with it.

What Psychology Says About Impatience:

Time Perception is Subjective: Research shows that our emotional state skews how we experience time. When we’re bored, stressed, or fixated on the outcome, time seems to crawl. This makes delays feel more unbearable than they actually are.

Impatience is Linked to Reward Circuits: Neuroscientific studies suggest that impatience is tied to the brain's dopaminergic reward system. When you anticipate a reward (a result, an answer, a success), dopamine surges. If that reward is delayed, the dopamine system flags an "error in prediction," triggering frustration.

Some People Are More Prone to Impatience: Personality traits like low distress tolerance, high impulsivity, or perfectionism make us more vulnerable to impatience. Additionally, if you often feel rushed or overwhelmed, your cognitive bandwidth is already strained—meaning even small delays can push you over the edge.

How to Calm Impatience When It Arises:

1. Name It to Tame It:

Say to yourself, "I’m feeling impatient right now." Labeling the emotion creates mental distance from the heat of the moment and activates the brain’s self-regulatory systems.

2. Shift Your Time Perspective:

Instead of focusing on the delay ("Why is this taking so long?"), shift to the present moment. Try grounding yourself with sensory input: What do you see? Hear? Feel? This brings your awareness into the now, where impatience loses its grip.

3. Reframe the Situation:

Ask yourself, "Is this a true emergency, or just uncomfortable?" Often, what feels urgent is just a challenge to our expectations. Reframing the moment as a test of patience can reduce the sense of threat.

4. Stretch Your Patience Muscle:

Like willpower, patience can be strengthened through practice. Set micro-challenges—wait 30 extra seconds before checking your phone, or let someone go ahead in line. Small acts of deliberate waiting train the brain to tolerate delay without triggering stress.

5. Investigate the Trigger:

After the moment passes, reflect: What exactly triggered my impatience? Was it the delay—or the feeling of being out of control, overlooked, or unproductive? Understanding the deeper emotional root helps prevent future flare-ups.

Final Thought:

Impatience isn't a flaw—it's a message. It tells you where your expectations, control, and tolerance are out of sync. The trick isn’t to eliminate impatience, but to respond to it with awareness instead of reactivity.

In a world that worships speed, reclaiming your ability to wait without distress is a quiet superpower. It builds emotional resilience, deepens your relationships, and helps you respond rather than react—the hallmark of psychological maturity.
