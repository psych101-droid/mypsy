The 1938 radio drama The War of the Worlds, directed and narrated by Orson Welles, is one of the most iconic broadcasts in American history. Based on H.G. Wells' 1898 novel, it aired on October 30, 1938, as part of The Mercury Theatre on the Air on CBS Radio. This Halloween episode famously simulated a live news broadcast, describing a Martian invasion of Earth, which was said to be occurring in real time.

The next day, newspapers, including The New York Times, reported widespread panic, claiming that the broadcast sparked hysteria among thousands of listeners who believed it to be a real event. The Times described “mass hysteria” gripping audiences between 8:15 and 9:30 p.m., as frantic listeners reportedly fled their homes, made farewell calls to loved ones, and even sought shelter in police stations. The portrayal of fear spread quickly, cementing the broadcast’s legacy as one of the most infamous media events in American history.

Hadley Cantril’s Study on Panic and Psychological Reactions

Two years after the broadcast, social psychologist Hadley Cantril published The Invasion from Mars: A Study in the Psychology of Panic. The work became groundbreaking in exploring the power of media and its psychological impact. Cantril estimated that around six million people heard the broadcast, with approximately one million experiencing genuine fear or distress. His research identified key psychological factors that contributed to the public’s response, detailing how individual biases, broadcast format, and social influences intersected to produce a range of reactions.

Cantril’s analysis highlighted several contributing factors:

  1. The Realistic Broadcast Format: By structuring the show as a series of “live” news bulletins, Welles’s team created an urgent and credible tone. This realism blurred the lines between fiction and reality, especially for listeners who tuned in after the program had started and missed the initial disclaimer that it was a dramatization.

  2. Audience Susceptibility and Individual Beliefs: Cantril noted that listeners already prone to anxiety or those who believed in extraterrestrial life were more likely to accept the broadcast as true. This predisposition affected how people processed the content, making them more susceptible to fear.

  3. Social Influence and Contagion: Word of mouth played a significant role in amplifying fear. Some listeners were influenced by family members or friends who believed the broadcast was real, reinforcing their fears. This social influence accelerated the spread of panic, with personal relationships and group dynamics shaping perceptions of threat.

  4. Cognitive Biases Under Stress: Cantril argued that the stress induced by the broadcast impaired critical thinking, leading some to react instinctively rather than rationally. In a heightened state of fear, many listeners took the news reports at face value, without questioning their plausibility.

Cantril’s study went on to impact fields beyond media studies, influencing theories on crowd psychology, information processing, and the psychology of fear. His work suggested that a mix of situational and psychological factors could make individuals more susceptible to media influence, especially when the information appears credible and provokes emotional responses.

Contemporary Perspectives: Was the Panic Overstated?

Over the years, many academics have challenged the view that mass hysteria followed the broadcast. More recent studies suggest that the extent of the panic was definitely exaggerated, with several factors contributing to this amplified story.

  1. Audience Reach and Competing Programs: Later analyses have shown that The War of the Worlds broadcast actually reached a smaller audience than initially believed. At the time, another popular radio program, The Chase and Sanborn Hour, was airing, drawing many listeners away from CBS. Surveys conducted afterward indicated that a majority of the public had either not heard the broadcast or had simply been unaffected by it.

  2. Media Sensationalism: Historians like W. Joseph Campbell argue that newspapers sensationalized the story, playing up the narrative of hysteria to portray radio as a dangerous medium. As print journalism was facing competition from the rapidly growing popularity of radio, this framing served to underscore the potential dangers of broadcast media. News outlets cited accounts of widespread fear, often based on little or no direct evidence, contributing to a myth of panic that quickly became embedded in public memory.

  3. Critiques of Cantril’s Methodology: Cantril’s research has faced scrutiny for methodological flaws, including a reliance on anecdotal evidence and interviews with a self-selected sample of listeners. Critics argue that Cantril’s data was skewed by focusing on those who had been directly affected or who had exaggerated their reactions after the fact. This selective sampling may have led to an overestimation of the broadcast’s impact.

  4. The Role of Social Amplification: Modern psychological studies suggest that much of the perceived panic may have been due to the spread of rumors and social reinforcement rather than first hand fear. Known as “contagion theory,” this phenomenon explains how secondhand accounts can shape perceptions, often creating an exaggerated sense of an event’s scope. Friends and family members may have discussed the broadcast, leading to rumors that spread far faster than the reality of the situation.

  5. Reassessing the Term “Panic”: Scholars such as Jefferson Pooley contend that while some listeners were indeed alarmed, the variety of responses ranged from amusement to skepticism. This perspective suggests that the concept of “mass hysteria” may oversimplify a complex set of reactions, which included a healthy amount of disbelief and humor.

In light of these modern insights, the narrative of widespread panic in response to The War of the Worlds radio broadcast seems less grounded in reality and more in media myth-making. Cantril’s study, while groundbreaking in its time, overstated the level of collective panic, influenced by his methodological choices and the contemporary cultural climate. However, this broadcast continues to stand as a powerful illustration of how media can influence public perception, with enduring lessons for today’s fast-paced media landscape.

Still Relevant Today

The War of the Worlds broadcast remains one of the most fascinating and contentious events in media history, one which left an indelible impact on our understanding of media psychology and audience behavior. While the initial response was reported to have been widespread hysteria, modern research has reframed it as a more complex mix of individual reactions and social influence, amplified by media sensationalism.
