With Valentine’s Day upon us, I considered writing a post on what psychology has to say about the science of love. Alternatively, as someone who is single, I also thought about writing an anti-Valentine’s Day post exploring the commodification of love and the unrealistic expectations surrounding commercially driven affection. Who said romance was dead? 😂

Instead, I’ve opted for a post about a fascinating psychology research paper that became synonymous with finding love but is actually about interpersonal closeness.

The paper in question, The Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness: A Procedure and Some Preliminary Findings, by Professor Arthur Aron and colleagues, was published in 1997 and presented a practical methodology for creating closeness. Pairs of strangers took turns answering a series of 36 questions that became progressively more personal, moving from relatively light topics to increasingly meaningful and emotionally revealing ones.

What The 36 Questions Were Designed To Do

The “36 questions” procedure was created as a research tool. The goal was methodological. If researchers want to understand what happens psychologically when people feel close, they can’t simply compare people who already share a close relationship with those who don’t. Existing partners bring their own history into the relationship. They’ve shared experiences, built memories, and chosen each other for particular reasons. All of that makes it hard to isolate what actually causes feelings of closeness. With this in mind, the 36-question procedure was developed to allow researchers to randomly assign strangers to experience closeness or not, and then examine its effects under controlled conditions.

The structure of the task was based on research into how people naturally become close, and three core principles guided its design:

Gradual, Reciprocal Self-Disclosure. Closeness develops when people share personal information, but not too much too quickly. Excessive intimacy early on can be off-putting. The procedure therefore uses a sequence of questions that increase in personal depth over time. Importantly, disclosure must be mutual. Participants take turns asking and answering, so vulnerability moves in both directions.

Responsiveness and Feeling Heard. The power of the task is not just in revealing personal information. It is also in feeling genuinely heard and responded to. The structured format creates space for attentiveness and responsiveness, which are central to the experience of closeness.

Perceived Similarity and Liking. Forming a bond also involves feeling that you share common ground and that the other person likes you. Perceived similarity often matters more than objective similarity. The questions therefore prompt participants to notice commonalities. They also include prompts that invite participants to express positive regard for one another. The design intentionally avoids inviting criticism or negative evaluation because the aim is to foster closeness efficiently.

In experimental settings, the 36 questions are presented in three sets of twelve. Participants are given approximately fifteen minutes per set and are encouraged to move at a natural pace rather than rushing. Most pairs typically complete only seven or eight questions per set within the allotted time.

On average, a substantial proportion of pairs who complete the procedure report feeling close to the person they interacted with.

The Study Goes Viral

Aron’s 36 questions study went viral in 2015, when a New York Times writer penned a story with the irresistible headline: “To Fall in Love With Anyone, Do This.” In the article, writer Mandy Len Catron recounted her own experience trying out the 36 questions with a college acquaintance.

In her TED Talk on the subject, Len Catron states that the study is designed to create romantic love in the laboratory (it’s not, it’s designed to create interpersonal closeness) and that the procedure involves two strangers asking each other 36 increasingly personal questions, who then stare into each other’s eyes without speaking for four minutes (the staring instruction is not part of the study’s procedure). Despite the slight misrepresentation of the original research, Len Catron’s story is a great one worth listening to.

Want To Give It A Go?

If you’re curious to try the 36 questions yourself, approach them in the right spirit.

Remember, this is not a guaranteed route to romance. The procedure was designed to create interpersonal closeness, not to manufacture love. While romantic attraction can sometimes emerge, that was never the goal of the original research. At its heart, this is simply a structured way of having a deeper conversation.

Treat it as something interesting to explore. A conversation experiment. A way to see what happens when two people deliberately move beyond small talk.

That said, a word of caution. Some of the questions become quite personal. For some people, certain topics may feel uncomfortable or even triggering. There should be absolutely no pressure to answer anything you don’t want to answer. You can skip a question. You can pause. You can stop entirely. Psychological safety matters.

If you do decide to try it, here’s how to structure it.

First, choose someone you’d like to feel closer to. This could be a partner, a friend, a colleague, a family member, or someone you’re just getting to know. Make sure both of you are genuinely comfortable sharing personal thoughts and feelings before you begin.

Set aside between 20 and 45 minutes when you can meet in person without interruptions.

Divide that time into three equal parts.

During the first third of your time, work through the questions in Set I. Take turns asking and answering. Both people should respond to each question, but alternate who answers first each time.

When the first third of your time is up, move on to Set II, even if you haven’t finished Set I. Again, take turns and alternate who goes first.

For the final third of your time, move to Set III, following the same format.

Each set is designed to become progressively more personal. Dividing the time evenly ensures that you spend roughly the same amount of time at each level of self-disclosure, rather than rushing through the early questions and lingering only on the later ones.

And that’s it.

No guaranteed outcomes. Just a structured conversation designed to explore what happens when two people choose to be a little more open than usual. Here are the questions:

Set I

1. Given the choice of anyone in the world, whom would you want as a dinner guest?

2. Would you like to be famous? In what way?

3. Before making a telephone call, do you ever rehearse what you are going to say? Why?

4. What would constitute a “perfect” day for you?

5. When did you last sing to yourself? To someone else?

6. If you were able to live to the age of 90 and retain either the mind or body of a 30-year-old for the last 60 years of your life, which would you want?

7. Do you have a secret hunch about how you will die?

8. Name three things you and your partner appear to have in common.

9. For what in your life do you feel most grateful?

10. If you could change anything about the way you were raised, what would it be?

11. Take four minutes and tell your partner your life story in as much detail as possible.

12. If you could wake up tomorrow having gained any one quality or ability, what would it be?

Set II

13. If a crystal ball could tell you the truth about yourself, your life, the future, or anything else, what would you want to know?

14. Is there something that you’ve dreamed of doing for a long time? Why haven’t you done it?

15. What is the greatest accomplishment of your life?

16. What do you value most in a friendship?

17. What is your most treasured memory?

18. What is your most terrible memory?

19. If you knew that in one year you would die suddenly, would you change anything about the way you are now living? Why?

20. What does friendship mean to you?

21. What roles do love and affection play in your life?

22. Alternate sharing something you consider a positive characteristic of your partner. Share a total of five items.

23. How close and warm is your family? Do you feel your childhood was happier than most other people’s?

24. How do you feel about your relationship with your mother?

Set III

25. Make three true “we” statements each. For instance, “We are both in this room feeling…”

26. Complete this sentence: “I wish I had someone with whom I could share…”

27. If you were going to become a close friend with your partner, please share what would be important for them to know.

28. Tell your partner what you like about them; be very honest this time, saying things that you might not say to someone you’ve just met.

29. Share with your partner an embarrassing moment in your life.

30. When did you last cry in front of another person? By yourself?

31. Tell your partner something that you like about them [already].

32. What, if anything, is too serious to be joked about?

33. If you were to die this evening with no opportunity to communicate with anyone, what would you most regret not having told someone? Why haven’t you told them yet?

34. Your house, containing everything you own, catches fire. After saving your loved ones and pets, you have time to safely make a final dash to save any one item. What would it be? Why?

35. Of all the people in your family, whose death would you find most disturbing? Why?

36. Share a personal problem and ask your partner’s advice on how they might handle it. Also, ask your partner to reflect back to you how you seem to be feeling about the problem you have chosen.

A Small Suggestion

If you try this more than once with different people, you may find your answers start to feel rehearsed. In that case, consider creating your own set of increasingly personal questions. If you do, feel free to share them in the comments. It would be great to build a bank of thoughtful prompts beyond the original 36.

Leave a comment

Whether you try the original 36 questions or create your own, may your conversations be honest, your listening generous, and your relationships grow a little closer as a result.
