This is an article about the psychology of appreciation, and our need to feel appreciated, so it seems only right to begin by appreciating the person who made me realise just how deep that need runs.

That person is the inimitable William James, who believed psychology should be relatable to people’s lives, thoughts, and lived experience. He was also, by any reasonable standard, one seriously cool dude. I mean, look at him. I wish I could pull off that look.

What makes this photo of James remarkable is that he was seriously ill when it was taken. During a scientific expedition to Brazil in 1865, James contracted varioloid, a form of smallpox, an illness that left him struggling with exhaustion, eye problems, digestive issues, insomnia, and episodes of deep psychological distress. At times, he was barely functioning. He worried he’d never be well again.

This period of illness changed everything. It pushed James away from a future as a naturalist and toward psychology. The rest, as they say, is history. He would go on to become one of the most influential figures in the field, culminating in the publication of The Principles of Psychology in 1890.

So, returning to the question of appreciation, here’s the precise moment James articulated the psychological importance of feeling appreciated.

In 1896, he received a small gift from his students at Radcliffe College: a potted azalea. James wrote back to thank them, and somewhere between the humor and the gratitude, he stumbled on a truth about being human.

Here’s the letter:

DEAR YOUNG LADIES,

I am deeply touched by your remembrance. It is the first time anyone ever treated me so kindly, so you may well believe that the impression on the heart of the lonely sufferer will be even more durable than the impression on your minds of all the teachings of Philosophy 2A. I now perceive one immense omission in my Psychology, - the deepest principle of Human Nature is the craving to be appreciated, and I left it out altogether from the book, because I had never had it gratified till now. I fear you have let loose a demon in me, and that all my actions will now be for the sake of such rewards. However, I will try to be faithful to this one unique and beautiful azalea tree, the pride of my life and delight of my existence. Winter and summer will I tend and water it—even with my tears. Mrs. James shall never go near it or touch it. If it dies, I will die too; and if I die, it shall be planted on my grave.

Don’t take all this too jocosely, but believe in the extreme pleasure you have caused me, and in the affectionate feelings with which I am and shall always be faithfully your friend,

WM. James.

The last line sent me off to the dictionary the first time I read it.

Jocosely means humorously, playfully, said in jest but not without sincerity. Which is exactly what this letter is. James is joking, exaggerating, performing a little. But he’s also being completely honest. Feeling appreciated matters!

Appreciation as a Psychological Construct

James’s remark works so well because it doesn’t reduce appreciation to politeness, manners, or even gratitude. What he’s describing is something broader and more psychologically fundamental: the experience of being noticed, valued, and emotionally recognised by others.

Modern psychology has since caught up with that intuition.

Rather than treating appreciation as a vague warm feeling, researchers now describe it as a distinct psychological construct. At its core, appreciation involves recognising the value of something and feeling a positive emotional connection to it. Importantly, that “something” isn’t limited to events or possessions. It can just as easily be a person.

This matters, because much of the popular conversation collapses appreciation into gratitude. The two are related, but they aren’t the same. Gratitude usually involves a clear benefactor, someone who has intentionally done something for us. Appreciation is wider. It includes gratitude, but it also captures experiences that don’t involve gifts, favors, or transactions at all.

One of the most important forms of appreciation, psychologically speaking, is interpersonal appreciation. This is the simple but powerful act of noticing and valuing the people in our lives. Not for what they’ve done for us, but for who they are, and for the role they play in our emotional world.

Interpersonal appreciation sounds obvious, but it’s surprisingly easy to overlook. We may feel fondness, affection, even love for the people around us, yet rarely articulate that sense of value in a way that’s felt. James’s reaction to the azalea captures this perfectly. The gift itself was modest. What moved him wasn’t the object, but what it signalled: that he had been seen, remembered, and valued.

Psychologically, feeling appreciated does something slightly different from feeling helped. It says, “You matter,” rather than, “You did something useful for me.” That distinction is subtle, but important. Being appreciated affirms our place in someone else’s inner world. It reassures us that we’re not invisible.

Research shows that feeling understood and appreciated by others is a major contributor to emotional closeness and connection. When people feel appreciated, they tend to feel more bonded, more secure, and more willing to invest in relationships. Appreciation, in this sense, isn’t just a pleasant extra. It’s one of the mechanisms through which relationships are built and maintained.

Seen this way, James’s letter stops looking like a charming historical curiosity and starts to look like a small psychological case study. In one unexpected moment of appreciation, he felt something he realised he’d been missing. Not admiration. Not praise. Just the personal confirmation that he mattered to someone else.

Spotting Appreciation in Psychological Theory

James wasn’t alone in identifying appreciation as something fundamental. Classic psychological theories place it squarely at the heart of human motivation and well-being, even if they use slightly different language to get there.

In Abraham Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the craving to be appreciated fits within the fourth level: esteem needs. This is the tier concerned with feeling valued, respected, and recognised by others, as well as developing a stable sense of self-respect. It’s the point at which survival and belonging give way to a deeper concern with how we’re seen.

Maslow was clear that esteem isn’t just about confidence or achievement. It also depends on external recognition. We need to feel that we matter, that our presence and contributions are acknowledged. Without that, people often struggle with feelings of inferiority, helplessness, or worthlessness. In other words, the absence of appreciation doesn’t just sting. It undermines how we experience ourselves.

A similar idea sits at the core of Carl Rogers’s person-centred theory, though he framed it in more relational terms. Rogers argued that human beings have a basic craving for affection and approval. This need isn’t superficial or indulgent. It’s central to personality development and psychological health.

One of Rogers’s most important concepts here is positive regard. From early in life, we’re sensitive to whether we’re accepted, valued, and appreciated by the people around us. When that regard is freely given, it supports healthy development. When it’s inconsistent or withheld, problems begin to emerge.

Rogers believed that many psychological difficulties stem from what he called conditions of worth. These develop when appreciation and affection are offered only on certain terms. Be successful. Be agreeable. Don’t feel too much. Don’t want the wrong things. Over time, people learn that being themselves isn’t enough. They start shaping their behavior around what earns approval, rather than what feels authentic.

The result is a growing gap between who someone really is and who they feel they have to be. Rogers saw this incongruence as a major source of distress, anxiety, and negative self-concept. In short, conditional appreciation comes at a psychological cost.

By contrast, Rogers argued that real growth occurs in the presence of unconditional positive regard. This is appreciation without strings attached. It’s the experience of being accepted and valued as a person, not evaluated or managed. When people feel appreciated in this way, they’re more likely to trust themselves, explore who they are, and move toward self-actualisation.

Seen through this lens, the need to feel appreciated isn’t a weakness or a desire for praise. It’s a deeply human requirement for psychological safety and growth. James’s azalea moment captures this beautifully. What moved him wasn’t flattery or status, but the simple, affirming message: you matter to us.

When Appreciation Expands Us

Appreciation doesn’t just shape how we develop a sense of self and relate to others. It also shapes what happens to us when we express it.

Appreciation is a wonderful thing. It makes what is excellent in others belong to us as well.

This great quote is most often attributed to Voltaire, and it captures something important about how appreciation works.

We usually think of excellence as belonging to the person who possesses it. Their talent. Their kindness. Their insight. Appreciation subtly changes that relationship. By noticing and valuing those qualities, we allow them to enrich our own inner world. We learn from them. We’re inspired by them. Sometimes, without quite realising it, we begin to cultivate similar qualities in ourselves.

There’s also a moral shift taking place here. Appreciation moves us away from envy and comparison. Instead of feeling diminished by someone else’s strengths, we’re enlarged by them. Their excellence stops being a threat and becomes something shared. Something human.

Psychologically, this is significant because appreciation trains attention. It teaches us what to look for. When our attention is habitually drawn to what’s admirable rather than what’s lacking, our experience of the social world changes. We become less defensive, less competitive, and more receptive. Over time, that way of seeing begins to shape character.

People who appreciate well often become more open, more grounded, and more generous in how they interpret others. Not because they ignore flaws or difficulties, but because their default orientation is toward value rather than deficiency.

So the quote isn’t suggesting we can claim other people’s achievements as our own. It’s pointing to something subtler. By recognising and valuing excellence wherever it appears, we allow it to live in us too. As understanding. As inspiration. As growth.

A Final Reflection

We often notice the good in the people in our lives. Their steadiness. Their generosity. Their patience. Their humor. But our appreciation often stays internal. We assume it’s obvious. We tell ourselves it doesn’t need saying. Or we save it for special occasions, as if everyday appreciation somehow doesn’t count.

James’s reaction to that small azalea suggests otherwise. What moved him wasn’t extravagance or praise, but the unexpected experience of being noticed and valued. A modest gesture carried disproportionate emotional weight because it spoke directly to a basic human need.

Psychology gives us good reason to take that seriously. Feeling appreciated supports self-worth, strengthens relationships, and fosters a sense of connection that’s hard to achieve in any other way. Expressing appreciation, meanwhile, doesn’t diminish us or inflate others unfairly. As Voltaire observed, it expands us. It sharpens attention, softens comparison, and draws us out of narrow self-focus.

Perhaps the most striking thing about appreciation is how little it costs and how much it offers. A few well-chosen words. A moment of recognition. A simple acknowledgment that someone matters.

If James was right that the craving to be appreciated runs deep in human nature, then it follows that offering appreciation is one of the most quietly powerful things we can do. Not dramatically. Not performatively. Just more often than we do now.

And if nothing else, his letter is a reminder that we may never know how much a small expression of appreciation might mean to someone else, until we choose to say it out loud.

And on that note, I appreciate you for taking the time to read this, and for supporting and being part of this community.
