When Psychology Meets Superhero Power:

Fact 1: Psychologist William Moulton Marston created and produced Wonder Woman under the pseudonym Charles Moulton.

Long before superheroes became cinematic icons, psychologist William Moulton Marston created one of the most enduring figures in comic book history: Wonder Woman. Writing under the pseudonym Charles Moulton, Marston blended his expertise in psychology with a talent for storytelling to bring this powerful character to life. His invention of Wonder Woman wasn’t just creative; it was personal. Marston had helped develop the first systolic blood pressure deception ('lie detector') test, which inspired Wonder Woman’s famous Lasso of Truth. With academic roots at Harvard and a strong interest in promoting feminist ideals, he imagined Wonder Woman as a hero who relied on empathy and intelligence rather than just brute force. It's a fascinating example of how psychological ideas can shape popular culture in lasting ways.

The Original Deep Talk

Fact 2: When Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung meet in person for the first time on the 3rd of March 1907, they talked for 13 hours without interruption.

When Carl Jung arrived at Sigmund Freud’s apartment at Berggasse 19 in Vienna on March 3, 1907, neither man expected that their first meeting would turn into a thirteen-hour conversation. They spoke without pause, covering ideas about the mind, the unconscious, dreams, and the future of psychology itself. This marathon exchange marked the beginning of a powerful intellectual relationship that would shape the early development of psychoanalysis. Although their friendship would eventually fracture, their first meeting was a remarkable moment in the history of psychology, filled with promise, mutual admiration, and intense curiosity.

How a Civil Rights Icon Called Psychologists to Action

Fact 3: Martin Luther King Jr. delivered a compelling address to the APA in September 1967.

On September 1, 1967, Nobel Prize–winning civil rights leader Martin Luther King Jr. spoke at the American Psychological Association’s Annual Convention in Washington, D.C. His speech, titled “The Role of the Behavioral Scientist in the Civil Rights Movement,” was a bold and stirring appeal for psychologists and behavioral scientists to take an active role in dismantling systemic injustice. He urged them to help facing issues like urban riots, the Vietnam War, rising unemployment, and the moral imperative of civil disobedience. King coined the idea of "creative maladjustment," encouraging social scientists to resist simply fitting into racist or unequal norms. He insisted that they speak truth to the white majority and help society understand how deeply racism corrupts its soul and structures.

The Original “IQ Insults”

Fact 4: Psychologist Henry H. Goddard proposed adopting the terms moron, imbecile, and idiot as three distinct classes of mental defect.

In May 1910, at the annual meeting of the American Association for the Study of the Feeble-Minded in Illinois, psychologist Henry H. Goddard introduced a system that classified levels of mental deficiency using terms we now recognize as offensive. He suggested that people with an IQ between 51 and 70 be labeled “moron,” those with scores between 26 and 50 be called “imbecile,” and those scoring below 25 be defined as “idiot.” Now pejorative terms no longer employed in a psychological context, moron, imbecile, and idiot were standard definitions within the field of 'mental retardation' for many years.

Although Goddard’s intention was to create a more precise vocabulary for mental testing, the categories carried damaging consequences. They helped fuel stereotypes about intelligence and were linked to harmful policies such as forced sterilization and widespread institutionalization.

The Dinner Party That Cooked Up Big Bird

Fact 5: Sesame Street began as a dinner party idea and went on to educate millions around the globe.

In 1966, at a dinner party, experimental psychologist Lloyd Morrisett asked television producer Joan Ganz Cooney whether television could be used to teach preschoolers. That simple question set in motion one of the most influential educational projects of the twentieth century. With Morrisett’s vision and Cooney’s production expertise, they launched the Children’s Television Workshop and developed a show designed to give disadvantaged children a stronger start in school.

Sesame Street premiered in the United States on November 10, 1969, proudly sponsored by the letters W, S, and E, and the numbers 2 and 3. From the very beginning, it stood apart because it was rooted in developmental psychology and formative research, blending entertainment with carefully designed lessons.

The show quickly became a cultural and educational phenomenon. Today it is broadcast in more than 140 countries, and it is estimated that over 77 million Americans watched the program as children. What began as a dinner party conversation grew into a global classroom that has shaped generations.
