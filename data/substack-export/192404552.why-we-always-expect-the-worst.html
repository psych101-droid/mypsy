As a Brit who feels guilty for writing in US English (I get fewer complaints about typos this way!) I saw a quote the other day that finally justified my decision: "I put the zing in catastrophizing." The UK English version, "I put the sing in catastrophising," doesn't work nearly as well. Anyway, that's what prompted this article!

I'll look at what catastrophizing is, what the research says about why we do it, and what we can do to reduce the anxiety when we start thinking the worst.

What is catastrophizing?

Pinning down a precise definition turns out to be quite tricky.

A widely cited formal definition comes from a 2001 paper by psychologist Michael Sullivan and colleagues, which described catastrophizing as:

An exaggerated negative mental set brought to bear during actual or anticipated pain experience.

This definition originated in pain research but has since been applied much more broadly.

Prior to this definition, two of psychology’s most influential figures had their own view on catastrophizing. Albert Ellis called it “awfulizing,” an irrational belief that a bad outcome is not just unpleasant but genuinely insurmountable. And Aaron Beck described it as an automatic cognitive error, a systematic bias in how the mind processes information rather than a deliberate choice.

Researchers still don’t agree on what catastrophizing fundamentally is. A review by Luana Petrini and Lars Arendt-Nielsen, concluded that there is no general theoretical consensus on the question. Is it a personality trait some people carry more than others? A situational state anyone can slip into under pressure? A cognitive schema, a kind of mental template? Or a coping strategy that serves some purpose, however counterproductive it might seem? Scientists hold all of these positions, and the debate hasn’t been settled.

The brain that defaults to the worst

If catastrophizing is a cognitive error, it’s a remarkably widespread one. A useful way to think about why comes from evolutionary psychology.

In a 2001 review published in the Review of General Psychology, Roy Baumeister and colleagues examined a deceptively simple question: are bad things psychologically more powerful than good things? Across everyday events, close relationships, learning, memory, emotion, health, and social support, the answer was consistently yes. Bad events, bad feedback, bad moods, and bad information all had greater impact than their positive equivalents. The brain that pays more attention to threats than to rewards isn’t a broken brain. It’s doing exactly what evolution shaped it to do. Organisms better attuned to danger were more likely to survive it, and more likely to pass on their genes.

The magnitude of this negativity bias varies by context. In close relationships and emotional recall, the gap between negative and positive experiences is particularly pronounced. The point isn’t a single precise ratio. It’s that the pattern is consistent and deep, and that the brain defaulting to worst-case thinking isn’t a character flaw. It’s the factory setting.

From this perspective, catastrophizing isn’t something that happens to certain people. It’s something the human brain is structurally primed to do, particularly when threat is present or when the future feels uncertain. Cognitive behavioural therapist and clinical psychologist Dr. Lucia Tecuta notes that before catastrophizing becomes chronic, it typically takes hold because it provides a perceived sense of control over unpredictable events. If you've already imagined the worst, so the thinking goes, you won't be caught off guard if the worst actually happens.

Research reviewed by Baumeister and colleagues suggests that a brain in mild threat-detection mode tends to process information more carefully and thoroughly than a contented one. The mind scanning for danger is being vigilant. It’s doing something functionally sophisticated, even when the threat turns out to be a slightly ambiguous text message, a look from a colleague that probably meant nothing, or a symptom that Google has already diagnosed as seventeen different things.

The case against the evolutionary reframe

Not everyone finds the evolutionary account convincing.

Ellis and Beck view catastrophizing not as an adaptive feature but as a cognitive distortion, an irrational belief or automatic error that causes unnecessary suffering and needs to be corrected. It could be argued, therefore, that framing worst-case thinking as a survival mechanism risks romanticizing something that is simply a mistake.

A more nuanced perspective comes from researchers, Ida Flink, Katja Boersma, and Steven Linton, who in a 2013 paper in Cognitive Behaviour Therapy, proposed that the causal arrow in catastrophizing may sometimes run in both directions. Rather than catastrophizing always driving distress, pre-existing distress may sometimes produce catastrophizing as a response. This is a theoretical proposition and hasn’t been confirmed for everyday catastrophizing in healthy adults.

Another perspective on the nature of catastrophizing comes from Eccleston and Crombez, whose misdirected problem-solving model, tested empirically by Flink et al, in a 2012 paper in the British Journal of Health Psychology, proposes that the catastrophizing mind isn't detecting threats so much as running a loop of worried thinking that fails to produce any actual solution. It's less a survival mechanism than a loop that keeps running, generating worry without ever producing an answer.

Where catastrophizing parts company with ordinary worry

The evolutionary account explains why we all default to scanning for threats. It doesn’t explain why some people’s imagined consequences go so much further than the evidence warrants. Research by Benjamin Hengen and Georg Alpers, suggests the gap lies not in how often we expect bad things to happen, but in what we imagine will happen when they do.

The research compared anxious and non-anxious individuals on two distinct questions: how often do you expect to encounter a threat, and how bad do you think it will be if you do? The finding was clear. Anxious people did not overestimate the frequency of encountering threats compared to non-anxious people. Their baseline sense of how often bad things happen was no more distorted than anyone else’s. The cognitive error occurred entirely in outcome estimation. Anxious people vastly overestimated the severity of what would happen if the encounter occurred.

The problem isn't that catastrophizers think bad things happen more often. It's that when they imagine bad things happening, the imagined consequences become far more catastrophic than the situation actually calls for. The frequency radar is calibrated roughly right. The severity dial is turned to maximum.

This is well worth bearing in mind, because it points to where any recalibration actually needs to happen. Not “how likely is this?” but “how bad would it actually be?”

What you can do when you catch yourself catastrophizing

Cognitive reappraisal, reframing a stressful event to reduce its emotional impact, has a substantial body of emotion regulation research behind it, most notably in James Gross's foundational work on reappraisal and emotional suppression.

The practical application of cognitive reappraisal relates directly to the Hengen and Alpers finding addressed in the last section. Namely, that when a catastrophic thought arrives, the question to examine isn’t whether something bad might happen, but how bad it would actually be, and whether you could cope with it if it did. Most catastrophic scenarios, examined carefully rather than felt intensely, turn out to involve outcomes that are unpleasant but manageable. The severity dial, once questioned, rarely survives scrutiny as well as it seemed to in the moment.

Recognizing the pattern of catastrophizing without pathologizing it is also supported by the research. Lucia Tecuta describes looking for thoughts that are rigid, black-and-white, and lacking in specific detail as markers of catastrophizing worth noticing. A catastrophic thought is typically vague when it comes to detail: “everything will fall apart,” “I won’t be able to handle it,” “it will be a disaster.” Pressing for specifics, what exactly would fall apart, what precisely would a disaster look like, tends to deflate the thought rather than amplify it.

Naming the thought as catastrophizing creates a small but useful cognitive distance between you and the spiral. Rather than experiencing the thought as reality, you’re observing it as a mental event, one the brain generates readily and routinely, for reasons that are understandable even when they’re not helpful. You're not dysfunctional. You're doing something every human brain is wired to do.

Two approaches that feel intuitive but have weaker support are worth mentioning. Trying to suppress a catastrophic thought, telling yourself firmly not to think about it, tends to have the opposite effect, making the thought more persistent rather than less. And seeking repeated reassurance, checking with others that the feared outcome won't occur, provides short-term relief but doesn't address the underlying severity estimation, and can entrench the pattern over time.

For anything beyond self-monitoring, my How to Stop Overthinking article covers the practical end of this territory in more depth.

A final thought

The brain that catastrophizes is doing something that our ancestors' lives depended on. The issue isn’t the threat-detection system. It’s that the system was set up for a world where most threats were immediate, physical, and resolvable by fight or flight, and we're now asking it to assess an ambiguous comment, a medical appointment, or an unanswered text message.

Whether that mismatch is something to correct or simply something to understand a little better, I'm genuinely not sure. But it helps to know that the problem isn't how often we expect bad things to happen. It's how catastrophic we imagine they'll be when they do.
